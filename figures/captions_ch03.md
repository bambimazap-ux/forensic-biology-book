# Chapter 3 – figure captions and anchors

## Figure 3.1 — Fig03-01_stages
Anchor: Section 3.1, after "…comparison follows once the profile itself has been assessed [Gil06, Sci21]."
Caption: **Figure 3.1. Stages of mixture interpretation: recognition, suitability, and order of comparison.** A structured framework illustrating the linked but distinct judgments in mixture analysis. (1) Mixture recognition evaluates signals across the full profile, distinguishing multiple contributors from technical artefacts while accounting for allele sharing and weak components that can conceal contributors. (2) Suitability assessment evaluates whether data meet validated laboratory criteria for comparison; the decision is not all-or-nothing across a profile and may support interpretation of the full profile, specific components, or a subset of loci, or result in an inconclusive determination. (3) The evidentiary profile is interpreted before reference profiles are considered, keeping candidate genotype combinations independent of knowledge about a person of interest. Source: Original illustration based on [Cla98, Gil06, Hu14, Sci21].

## Figure 3.2 — Fig03-02_noc-mac
Anchor: Section 3.2, after "…no participating laboratory classified it as a two-person mixture [But18]."
Caption: **Figure 3.2. Number of contributors (NoC) and the lower-bound property of maximum allele count (MAC).** (A) Allele sharing conceals contributors at a single locus. Heterozygous individuals carry two alleles, but overlapping alleles between contributors reduce the number of distinct observed peaks; observing four alleles establishes that at least two contributors are present, but can arise from two, three, or four contributors. (B) Across multiple loci in a simulated multi-person mixture constructed so that no locus shows more than four alleles, as described for MIX13 Case 5 [But18]; per-locus counts are invented for illustration. The maximum allele count across the profile (MAC = 4) provides a lower bound indicating at least two contributors, rather than a definitive count of everyone who contributed; multi-locus evaluation and maximum-likelihood methods evaluate patterns across loci. Simulated data for illustration. Source: Original illustration based on [Pao05, Han11, Per11, But18, Sci21].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive contributor slider showing how increasing contributors from 2 to 4 increases allele sharing and alters observed peak counts across loci).

## Figure 3.3 — Fig03-03_major-minor
Anchor: Section 3.3, after "…rather than inferred from one peak or a universal numerical rule [Gil06, Lec04, But06]."
Caption: **Figure 3.3. Major and minor components, heterozygote balance, and mixture proportion.** Simulated electropherogram of a two-person mixture showing relative signal at two informative loci. (A) At a four-allele locus with no allele sharing, a balanced pair of tall peaks forms the major component (1) and a balanced pair of short peaks forms the minor component (2); within-component heterozygote balance allows candidate allele pairing and assessment of mixture proportion—the relative signal attributed to a component, not a direct measurement of the amount of DNA originally contributed by each person. (B) At a three-allele locus, sharing of an allele (here, allele 20) combines signal from both contributors (3), producing a peak taller than the unshared major allele; the peak height reflects composite signal rather than a single individual's DNA amount. Peaks shown are above the analytical threshold (4, both panels). Peak heights and threshold positions are illustrative and do not represent a specific kit; refer to Figure 2.5 for stutter, imbalance, and threshold features. Simulated data for illustration. Source: Original illustration based on [Cla98, Gil98b, Gil06, Lec04, But06].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive component slider adjusting contributor signal and dynamically demonstrating shared-allele signal stacking).

## Figure 3.4 — Fig03-04_deconvolution
Anchor: Section 3.4, after "…not a claim that every higher-order mixture is impossible to interpret by any method [Lyn18]."
Caption: **Figure 3.4. Genotype deconvolution, conditioning on a known contributor, and combinatorial complexity.** (A) Deconvolution at an illustrative three-allele locus (alleles 12, 14, 16). Unconditioned deconvolution evaluates all candidate genotype pairings for Contributor 1 and Contributor 2; relative signal and balance rule out conflicting pairings, but multiple alternatives may remain plausible. Conditioning on a known contributor (e.g. assuming a person, such as a victim, has genotype 12, 14) constrains the remaining possibilities for the unknown contributor to genotypes containing the unexplained allele (16), substantially narrowing the candidate set; conditioning is an interpretive assumption, not independent proof of presence or physical separation. Comparing a reference profile with the mixture is not the same as conditioning on a known contributor (Section 3.4). (B) Qualitative schematic of combinatorial complexity growth as the number of contributors increases from two to three and four. Possible genotype allocations multiply as contributors increase. Lynch and Cotton argue that manual deconvolution should generally not be attempted for more than two people unless a clear major contributor is identifiable; this is not a claim that such mixtures cannot be interpreted by any method [Lyn18]. Source: Original illustration based on [Cla98, Gil06, Hu14, Lyn18].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive deconvolution matrix allowing user to toggle known contributor genotypes and filter remaining candidate pairings).
