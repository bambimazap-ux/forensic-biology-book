## Figure 5.1 — Fig05-01_database-architectures
Anchor: Section 5.1, after "…allele designations and data formats also affect reliable exchange [But12d, But15g]."
Caption: **Figure 5.1. Forensic DNA database architectures, functional indices, and marker panel compatibility.** (A) National and cross-border database architectures. Databases operate as centralised national collections (e.g. UK NDNAD), hierarchical federated networks (US CODIS linking local, state, and national tiers [But12d]), or linked decentralised networks (the Prüm model, featuring automated step-1 hit/no-hit profile searches followed by bilateral step-2 requests for personal and case data [Ama19c, Too19]). International bodies also maintain shared repositories (INTERPOL Gateway, Europol) [Ama19c]. (B) Functional index segregation. Systems maintain separate indices for known-person records, crime-scene forensic traces, and missing-person/unidentified remains comparisons [Ge14b, But12d]. Personnel elimination databases operate as a separate quality-assurance layer for contamination control rather than criminal investigation [Nog25]. (C) Marker panel overlap. Core reference panels (CODIS core loci and the European Standard Set, ESS) share common loci, but differing configurations or older profiles yield partial overlap, constraining direct comparison [But12d, But15g]. Source: Original illustration based on [Ge14b, But12d, Ama19c, But15g, Nog25, Too19].

## Figure 5.2 — Fig05-02_mixture-database-searching
Anchor: Section 5.3, after "…not a verified contributor list [Ben17c, Noz24]."
Caption: **Figure 5.2. Mixture database searching: matching-allele count versus likelihood-ratio ranking.** (A) Searching mixtures against databases. Traditional retrieval criteria based on matching-allele counts or fixed-mismatch rules leave candidate profiles with identical counts tied in an unranked block. Likelihood-ratio searching tools (e.g. SmartRank, probabilistic-genotyping searches [Ben17c, Noz24]) evaluate candidate records as potential contributors, assigning a likelihood ratio to order candidates that meet a retrieval criterion. (B) Retrieval threshold trade-offs. The chosen cutoff determines entry onto the candidate list; the LR determines candidate order. A lower cutoff retains more true contributors but increases candidate list size and laboratory review workload; a stricter cutoff reduces false leads but risks omitting true contributors with drop-out [Ben17c]. (C) Evidential status. A ranked candidate list is a prioritised worklist for laboratory review and case follow-up, not a verified contributor list [Ben17c, Noz24]. A hit is an investigative lead, not an identification [But12d]. Simulated data for illustration. Source: Original illustration based on [Bri14e, But12d, Ben17c, Noz24].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive threshold simulator demonstrating how adjusting the LR retrieval cutoff expands or contracts the candidate worklist, showing true contributor retention versus candidate review workload).

## Figure 5.3 — Fig05-03_database-search-problem
Anchor: Section 5.4, after "…evidential evaluation requires a comparison under stated propositions [But24b]."
Caption: **Figure 5.3. Search statistics, all-pairs comparisons, and the database search problem.** (A) Directed comparison versus database search. A named-suspect comparison evaluates evidence given propositions about that individual; a database search selects a candidate from among $N$ records, meaning the search process and prior information shape the inference [Mee19, Bal95]. (B) All-pairs comparison paradox. Searching a single trace against a database involves $N$ comparisons. In contrast, comparing all pairs within a database involves $N(N - 1)/2$ pairwise comparisons, scaling quadratically. In large databases, partial matches sharing many loci between unrelated people become mathematically inevitable across billions of comparisons; this does not represent the probability that a specific query trace will adventitiously match a record [Wei04, Mee19]. (C) Evidential formulations. Evidential weight depends on propositions and search context: a database-centred formulation ($1/(np)$) reflects an open search where all members are equally plausible donors, whereas a suspect-centred formulation ($1/p$) applies when a candidate was already targeted or alternatives are excluded [Mee19]. Simulated data for illustration. Source: Original illustration based on [Mee19, Bal95, Wei04, But24b].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive comparison calculator showing linear $N$ vs quadratic $N(N-1)/2$ scaling alongside candidate evidential formulations $1/p$ and $1/(np)$ across varying database sizes).

## Figure 5.4 — Fig05-04_familial-searching-workflow
Anchor: Section 5.5, after "…statistical controls do not settle this policy balance [Kim11c, Mat20c, Piq19]."
Caption: **Figure 5.4. Familial searching: multi-stage workflow, candidate triage, and lineage/demographic constraints.** (A) Multi-stage investigative workflow. Familial searching proceeds through: (1) administrative qualification (serious unsolved cases, exhausted routine searches, formal authorisation [Piq19]); (2) candidate ranking using kinship likelihood ratios or allele sharing [Kim11c, Ge20]; (3) triage thresholds balancing pool size against relative retention; (4) secondary lineage marker testing (Y-STR or mtDNA) for lineage concordance [Kim11c, Ge20]; (5) pedigree and public-record investigative follow-up; and (6) direct reference profile comparison with the identified suspect [Kim11c]. (B) Biological and lineage constraints. Lineage markers cannot distinguish among patrilineal relatives sharing a Y-haplotype; distant relatives may appear as apparent close leads [Roh13]; and population substructure can impede separation of relatives from unrelated individuals [Roh12]. (C) Governance and demographic impacts. Demographic overrepresentation in databases subjects relatives of profiled individuals to disproportionate investigative scrutiny [Roh13, Kim11c, Mat20c]. Familial searching generates an investigative lead, not an identification [Piq19, Ge20]. Source: Original illustration based on [Piq19, Ge20, Kim11c, Roh12, Roh13, Mat20c].

## Figure 5.5 — Fig05-05_investigative-attrition-funnel
Anchor: Section 5.6, after "…and an associated outcome does not establish causation."
Caption: **Figure 5.5. Database performance measures and the investigative attrition funnel.** (A) Metric misalignment. Database size and profile counts measure capacity and coverage, not success. Hit rates depend on the denominator chosen (crime-scene stains, person records, or population), and expansive inclusion models do not reliably yield more matches per included person [San13b]. (B) Warm versus cold hits. A warm hit confirms a person already under investigation; a cold hit provides a novel investigative lead. Aggregate reporting often conflates these categories [Ama19, Ama21]. (C) The investigative attrition funnel. A database hit is an intermediate event, not a solved case. Cases progress through crime-scene loading, search, candidate hit, laboratory confirmation, police investigation, charge, and judicial conviction, experiencing attrition at each stage [Ama19, Ama21, Str19b]. Each stage has a distinct denominator, and an associated justice outcome does not establish causation. Simulated data for illustration. Source: Original illustration based on [San13b, Str19b, Ama19, Ama21, Wic22].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive attrition funnel simulator allowing user to explore case dropout rates across each transition from crime-scene stain to judicial conviction, contrasting warm vs cold hits).
