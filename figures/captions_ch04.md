# Chapter 4 – figure captions and anchors

## Figure 4.1 — Fig04-01_theta-sampling
Anchor: Section 4.2, after "…they do not remove either source of uncertainty [Bod16]."
Caption: **Figure 4.1. Population substructure ($\theta$) versus finite-database sampling uncertainty.** (A) Population substructure and coancestry. The product rule presumes independent allele draws from a population; shared ancestry within subgroups induces correlation between allele copies. In the Balding–Nichols model, the coancestry parameter $\theta$ ($F_{ST}$) adjusts genotype probabilities to allow for this population-level structure. The parameter $\theta$ represents background population correlation and does not replace an explicit relationship model for a known close relative [Bal94]. (B) Finite-database sampling uncertainty. Drawing a finite reference sample from a target population yields frequency estimates subject to sampling variation, quantified by confidence or credible intervals [Cur02, Cur05]. Sampling intervals do not model population structure, and a minimum-frequency floor does not correct for substructure [Bod16]. Simulated data for illustration. Source: Original illustration based on [Bal94, Bal05b, Cur02, Cur05, Bod16].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive $\theta$ slider adjusting genotype probabilities under the Balding–Nichols model alongside sampling confidence intervals).

## Figure 4.2 — Fig04-02_rmp-vs-lr
Anchor: Section 4.3, after "…without changing the observed profile [But24b]."
Caption: **Figure 4.2. Random match probability (RMP), likelihood ratio (LR), and the restricted conditions for equivalence.** (A) Profile rarity versus evidential weight. The random match probability describes profile rarity in a specified population under genetic assumptions, whereas the likelihood ratio evaluates the relative probability of the observed DNA findings under competing propositions ($H_p$ vs. $H_d$). Profile rarity alone is not evidential weight [Bal95, But24b]. See Figure 0.2 for likelihood ratio logic and the transposed-conditional error. (B) The likelihood ratio may approximate $1/\mathrm{RMP}$ only under four restricted conditions: (1) a full single-source profile, (2) the person of interest is assumed to be the source under $H_p$ ($\Pr(E \mid H_p) \approx 1$), (3) laboratory error is set aside, and (4) the alternative proposition specifies an unrelated person from the specified population ($\Pr(E \mid H_d) \approx \mathrm{RMP}$) [For03, Bal95]. Equivalence breaks down when alternative propositions specify a close relative (increasing $\Pr(E \mid H_d)$ and reducing the LR) or when conditioning information changes, altering the LR without changing the profile's RMP [Bal95, But24b]. Source: Original illustration based on [For03, Bal95, But24b, Hic22, Gil18].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive hypothesis selector comparing unrelated vs. sibling alternative propositions, demonstrating how $\Pr(E \mid H_d)$ increases and the likelihood ratio decreases while the profile's RMP remains unchanged).

## Figure 4.3 — Fig04-03_pedigree-likelihoods
Anchor: Section 4.5, after "…it does not prove the relationship [Ege00, Ge10]."
Caption: **Figure 4.3. Kinship and pedigree likelihoods in missing-person and disaster-victim identification.** (A) Missing-person pedigree comparison (illustrative pedigree). Profiles from multiple typed relatives (solid symbols) are evaluated jointly with untyped relatives (open symbols with question marks) through Mendelian inheritance links. The likelihood of the genetic findings from unidentified human remains is evaluated under competing propositions: $H_p$ (remains occupy the specified position in the pedigree) versus $H_d$ (remains are from an unknown, unrelated person, or from a different relationship) [Ge10]. (B) Principles of pedigree evaluation: (1) a specified mutation model allows a genuine relative to remain compatible despite an apparent mismatch [Gje07, Ge10]; (2) the best-supported pedigree is bounded by the candidate pedigree set specified, and omitted relationships can change rankings [Ege00]; and (3) in disaster-victim identification (DVI), joint assignment of multiple remains avoids mutually incompatible individual matches, conditional on the family pedigrees used [Vig21]. Source: Original illustration based on [Ge10, Gje07, Ege00, Vig21, Pri07, Mor02].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive pedigree allowing user to toggle typed versus untyped family members and observe the effect on likelihood support and pedigree ranking).

## Figure 4.4 — Fig04-04_mixture-models
Anchor: Section 4.7, after "…models that do not directly evaluate peak heights."
Caption: **Figure 4.4. Comparison of mixture interpretation approaches: binary, semi-continuous, and continuous models.** A three-column comparison of statistical models for mixture interpretation. Binary models (CPI/RMNE) evaluate allele presence or absence above the analytical threshold, discarding peak heights and requiring exclusion of loci where drop-out is reasonably possible [Bie16f, Cob19]. Semi-continuous models (e.g. LRmix Studio, Lab Retriever) assign validation-based probabilities to drop-out and drop-in events and average over possible genotypes weighted by population frequencies, without directly modelling quantitative peak heights in the likelihood [Gil12, Cob19, Inm15]. Continuous models (probabilistic genotyping: EuroForMix, DNAStatistX, STRmix) use quantitative peak heights alongside allele designations, modelling peak-height variation, stutter, degradation, and drop-out to distinguish genotype configurations that share the same called alleles [Cow07, Man20b, Gil21c]. Computational distinctions (gamma maximum-likelihood estimation vs. Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo) reflect broad algorithmic differences, not a guarantee that implementations are interchangeable [Gil21c]. Source: Original illustration based on [Gil06, Bie16f, Gil12, Cob19, Inm15, Cow07, Cow13, Gil21c, Bri15b, But24b].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive selector toggling between binary, semi-continuous, and continuous models on an electropherogram, showing how peak heights and drop-out probabilities are progressively incorporated into the likelihood calculation).

## Figure 4.5 — Fig04-05_tppr-framework
Anchor: Section 4.8, after "…consequently, the same finding may be expected under more than one account [Tay18, Oor19, Tay21]."
Caption: **Figure 4.5. The TPPR framework: from activity to recovered DNA findings.** (A) Evaluation of DNA evidence given activity-level propositions follows the physical path from human activity to observation through transfer, persistence, prevalence, and recovery (TPPR). (1) Transfer concerns movement between people and objects, encompassing direct contact as well as indirect (secondary or tertiary) transfer via intermediaries [Tay18, Oor19]. (2) Persistence concerns whether biological material remains available over time prior to collection [Tay18, Tay21]. (3) Prevalence addresses the possibility of relevant material already being present in the background [Oor19, Tay21]. (4) Recovery concerns what the collection and analytical process detects [Tay18, Gil25b]. Because transfer, persistence, and background can combine along multiple pathways, the same observed DNA profile may be expected under more than one account [Tay18, Oor19, Tay21]. (B) Evidentiary principles: a sub-source likelihood ratio addresses who contributed DNA but does not establish how or when it was deposited [Gil20]; experimental recovery data show substantial variation among laboratories (as demonstrated in the ReAct study [Gil25b]); and Bayesian networks formalise these dependencies, pathways, and assumptions [Tay18b, Tay21, Sam20]. Source: Original illustration based on [Coo98, Gil20, Tay18, Oor19, Tay21, Gil25b, Tay18b, Sam20, Buc25].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive pathway simulator allowing user to adjust transfer mode, elapsed persistence time, and background prevalence to observe how expected profile findings shift under competing activity propositions).
