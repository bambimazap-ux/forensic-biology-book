### Figure 7.1

- **File**: `Fig07-01_mitogenome-architecture.png` / `Fig07-01_mitogenome-architecture.svg`
- **Script**: `Fig07-01_mitogenome-architecture.py`
- **Width**: Full text width (`FULL_W`, 16.0 cm)
- **Placement anchor**: Section 7.1, immediately following:
  > "...A match therefore supports correspondence to a maternal lineage, not unique identification of one contributor; people without a known maternal relationship may also share a sequence [Amo19b, Car26b]."
- **Caption**:
  > **Figure 7.1. Mitochondrial genome architecture, cellular abundance, and maternal-line inheritance.** (A) Circular human mitochondrial genome (16,569 bp). The non-coding Control Region (regulatory D-loop containing hypervariable segments HVS-I, HVS-II, and HVS-III) holds primary forensic sequence variation, surrounded by a compact coding region (~15.5 kb) containing 13 oxidative phosphorylation subunit genes, 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs [Cou21b, Amo19b]. (B) Cellular copy-number advantage: hundreds to thousands of mtDNA molecules per cell enable recovery from scarce or degraded templates (rootless hair shafts, teeth, weathered bones) where diploid nuclear DNA (2 copies/cell) fails [Cou21b, Car26b]. Crucial rule: high copy number improves recovery prospects rather than individual-level specificity [Car26b]. (C) Matrilineal transmission: mothers transmit mtDNA to all offspring (sons and daughters), but only daughters pass it onward to descendants; males are reproductive dead ends for mtDNA transmission [Amo19b, Cou21b]. All matrilineal kin share the same basic haplotype across generations. (D) Core evidentiary principle: a match supports correspondence to a maternal lineage, not unique individualisation; unrelated individuals in the population may also share common haplotypes by chance, and nuclear autosomal comparison remains required for individual attribution [Amo19b, Car26b]. Simulated data for illustration. Source: Original illustration based on [Cou21b, Amo19b, Car26b].

### Figure 7.2

- **File**: `Fig07-02_sequencing-strategies.png` / `Fig07-02_sequencing-strategies.svg`
- **Script**: `Fig07-02_sequencing-strategies.py`
- **Width**: Full text width (`FULL_W`, 16.0 cm)
- **Placement anchor**: Section 7.2, immediately following:
  > "...Casework use depends on validation of the specific platform and end-to-end workflow for relevant samples; the FBI study supports its control-region assay, Holt et al. validate the specified kit–instrument–software workflows, and the missing-person study reports gains and limitations relative to its laboratory’s Sanger method [Bra19, Hol21b, Cue20]."
- **Caption**:
  > **Figure 7.2. Target regions and sequencing strategies: Sanger, MPS tiled amplicons, and hybridization capture.** (A) Forensic target scopes: targets expand from compact control-region segments (HVS-I/HVS-II, ~610 bp) to the full control region (1,122 bp, adding HVS-III and intervening sites) and the complete mitogenome (16,569 bp, adding coding variants and phylogenetic resolution) [Cou21b, Tem13, Car26b]. Whole-mitogenome matches remain shared among maternal relatives [Cou21b]. (B) Sequencing technologies: (1) Sanger sequencing uses chain termination across PCR amplicons, standard for HVS-I/II but labour-intensive across the whole genome [Cou21b, Car26b]; (2) MPS tiled amplicons utilize multiplex PCR of short overlapping products to span the control region or whole genome, avoiding reliance on single long PCR amplicons in compromised samples [Hol21b, Bra19]; (3) MPS hybridization capture uses biotinylated complementary probes to enrich target fragments from damaged libraries without intact PCR templates, recovering complete mitogenomes from severely degraded skeletal remains [Tem13, Cue20]. (C) Casework trade-offs: higher sensitivity and broader coverage demand platform-validated end-to-end workflows; analytical performance does not automatically transfer across platforms or kits [Bra19, Hol21b, Cue20]. Source: Original illustration based on [Cou21b, Tem13, Car26b, Hol21b, Bra19, Cue20].

### Figure 7.3

- **File**: `Fig07-03_contamination-numts-damage.png` / `Fig07-03_contamination-numts-damage.svg`
- **Script**: `Fig07-03_contamination-numts-damage.py`
- **Width**: Full text width (`FULL_W`, 16.0 cm)
- **Placement anchor**: Section 7.3, immediately following:
  > "...Phylogenetic consistency checks can prompt review of unexpected patterns but cannot replace examination of reads and controls [Grz18, Car26b]."
- **Caption**:
  > **Figure 7.3. Contamination controls, confounding artefacts (NUMTs and post-mortem damage), and quality assurance.** (A) Contamination safeguards: high sensitivity demands strict spatial separation of pre- and post-amplification work areas, dedicated reagents, extraction reagent blanks, negative amplification controls, and staff elimination databases to deconvolve potential laboratory carryover [Par14c, Grz18]. Signal in a blank requires contextual case evaluation [Grz18]. (B) Confounding molecular artefacts: (1) nuclear mitochondrial DNA segments (NUMTs) are ancient pseudogenes translocated into nuclear chromosomes that can co-amplify and mimic heteroplasmy or point substitutions, with no single read-level feature being decisive [Mar21d, Car26b]; (2) post-mortem hydrolytic damage produces spontaneous cytosine deamination ($C \rightarrow T$ transitions, and $G \rightarrow A$ on complementary strands) enriched at fragment ends in degraded DNA, distinguishable by damage patterns and replication failure across independent amplifications [Mar21d, Cou21b]. (C) Multi-tier quality assurance: replicate sequencing across strands or primers, independent expert raw data review, electronic data transfer to eliminate manual entry errors, phylogenetic consistency checks, and regular proficiency testing [Par14c, Grz18]. Source: Original illustration based on [Cou21b, Car26b, Grz18, Par14c, Mar21d].

### Figure 7.4

- **File**: `Fig07-04_heteroplasmy-tissue-segregation.png` / `Fig07-04_heteroplasmy-tissue-segregation.svg`
- **Script**: `Fig07-04_heteroplasmy-tissue-segregation.py`
- **Width**: Full text width (`FULL_W`, 16.0 cm)
- **Placement anchor**: Section 7.4, immediately following:
  > "...In a multigenerational whole-mitogenome study, treating heteroplasmic variants as differences excluded known maternal relatives, whereas guideline-based treatment avoided false exclusions in that pedigree [Con22b]."
- **Caption**:
  > **Figure 7.4. Point and length heteroplasmy, developmental tissue segregation, and interpretation guidelines.** (A) Heteroplasmy classes: (1) point heteroplasmy (PHP) manifests as alternative bases at a single site (e.g. 16169 C/T), appearing as double peaks in Sanger CE or proportional read counts in MPS [Par14c, Stu19]; (2) length heteroplasmy (LHP) appears as length distributions in homopolymeric cytosine tracts, causing out-of-phase sequencing traces [Par14c, Stu19]. (B) Developmental tissue segregation: mitochondrial bottlenecking during embryogenesis causes variant proportions to vary across tissues (e.g. buccal reference vs hair shaft) and among individual hairs from the same person [Gaa20]. Variants minor in a reference (e.g. 10% T) can predominate in single shed hairs (e.g. 65% T), demonstrating how an apparent hair–reference difference can arise without a difference in maternal lineage [Gaa20]. (C) Analytical thresholds: Sanger detects variants above ~10–20%, while MPS detects lower-level variants (~1–5%) but requires validated thresholds to avoid technical noise [Par14c, Gaa20]. (D) Core interpretation standard: heteroplasmic differences or a single sequence discrepancy do not automatically exclude shared maternal kinship; treating heteroplasmy as an exclusion causes false exclusions of true relatives [Par14c, Con22b]. Simulated data for illustration. Source: Original illustration based on [Par14c, Stu19, Gaa20, Ewe26, Con22b].

### Figure 7.5

- **File**: `Fig07-05_empop-alignment-weight.png` / `Fig07-05_empop-alignment-weight.svg`
- **Script**: `Fig07-05_empop-alignment-weight.py`
- **Width**: Full text width (`FULL_W`, 16.0 cm)
- **Placement anchor**: Section 7.6, immediately following:
  > "...The alternative source may be a maternal relative who shares the haplotype, so a match supports lineage correspondence but generally cannot distinguish among people with that haplotype [Buc11, And21b]."
- **Caption**:
  > **Figure 7.5. Alignment conventions, phylogenetic nomenclature, EMPOP database querying, and evidential weight.** (A) Reference coordinates and nomenclature: sequences are reported relative to the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS) [And99], a reporting standard rather than an ancestral sequence [Par14c]. Notation captures substitutions (73G), insertions (315.1C), deletions (249del), and IUPAC codes [Par14c]. (B) Homopolymer alignment and SAM2: repetitive cytosine tract indels are placed inconsistently under 3' vs phylogenetic alignment [Ban06]; SAM2 resolves artificial mismatches through alignment-independent string searches [Hub18, Par07d]. Haplogroup motifs are validated against PhyloTree [Dur21]. (C) EMPOP population framework: searches return observed counts $x/N$; zero observations ($x=0$) do not mean absence from the population, requiring upper confidence limits or Bayesian adjustments [Par14c, Buc11]. (D) Evidential weight and propositions: the likelihood ratio evaluates evidence under prosecution proposition $H_p$ versus alternative-source propositions $H_d$ that account for shared maternal relatives [Buc11, And21b]. An mtDNA match supports lineage correspondence, not individualization, and the likelihood ratio must never be transposed into the probability that the defendant is the source [Par14c, Buc11]. Source: Original illustration based on [And99, Par14c, Ban06, Hub18, Par07d, Dur21, Par07c, Buc11, And21b].
