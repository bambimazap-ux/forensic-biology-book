# Chapter 1 – figure captions and anchors

## Figure 1.1 — Fig01-01_testing-decision-path
Anchor: Section 1.1, after "…neither label establishes that an entire stain consists of one fluid, identifies its donor, or explains how it was deposited [Sij21, Cut24]."
Caption: **Figure 1.1. Decision logic, limitations, and inferential boundaries in body-fluid testing.** The tiered analytical pathway across three stages: (1) Locating stains (visual inspection and alternative light sources [ALS] provide leads for testing, but substrate colour or fluorescent materials can mask or imitate signals [Sij21]); (2) Presumptive screening (rapid catalytic or enzymatic tests indicate a possible target, but are vulnerable to plant materials, metal salts, and cleaning products [Tob07, Ven14]); and (3) Confirmatory or target-specific testing (lateral-flow immunochromatography or microscopy supports the presence of a defined target within validated limits [Cut24, Gam20]). A negative result does not necessarily exclude the target fluid. Downstream test order and material use are coordinated with DNA analysis (Chapter 2) [Tob07]. A positive fluid test establishes only the presence of the detected target substance within validated limits, without identifying donor identity or deposition circumstances [Sij21, Cut24]. Source: Original illustration based on [Sij21, Cut24, Mor21b, Str23b, Ven14, Tob07].
Interactive candidate: yes (interactive decision path where toggling test outcomes displays supported forensic inferences vs. unsupported claims).

## Figure 1.2 — Fig01-02_blood-test-mechanisms
Anchor: Section 1.2, after "…RSID-Blood targets glycophorin A, a protein associated with human red-cell membranes [Cut24]."
Caption: **Figure 1.2. Mechanisms of catalytic presumptive tests and immunochromatographic assays for blood.** (A) Catalytic presumptive tests: heme in haemoglobin catalyses oxidation of a colour-producing reagent in the presence of an oxidant (e.g. Kastle-Meyer, leucomalachite green) or light emission in luminol [Bar07, Tob07]. Catalytic tests can be affected by plant materials, metal salts, and cleaning products [Tob07, Ven14]; luminol chemiluminescence can be produced by rust, plant peroxidases, bleach, and certain soils or paints [Bar07]. (B) Lateral-flow immunochromatography (targeting human haemoglobin or glycophorin A): extract migrates along the membrane; dye-labelled antibodies bind the target antigen and form a visible line at the test area (T) and control area (C) [Cut24]. (1) Negative result (antigen not detected; does not necessarily exclude blood [Cut24]); (2) Positive result (supports presence of defined target [Cut24]); and (3) High-dose hook effect, where very high antigen concentration impairs antibody binding, causing a weak or absent test line; dilution of the extract is a suggested action [Cut24]. Source: Original illustration based on [Tob07, Ven14, Bar07]; panel (B) adapted from [Cut24].
Interactive candidate: yes (antigen concentration simulation demonstrating normal test line formation, negative outcome, and false-negative hook effect).

## Figure 1.3 — Fig01-03_semen-components-targets
Anchor: Section 1.3, after "…conversely, a positive protein assay indicates a marker, not sperm-cell presence [Gam20, Sak20]."
Caption: **Figure 1.3. Semen components, anatomical origins, and forensic targets.** (A) Semen fractions: seminal plasma consists largely of accessory-gland secretions, containing acid phosphatase (AP; high enzyme activity, presumptive colour test, not specific to semen [Gam20]), prostate-specific antigen (PSA/p30; prostate-derived, useful when cells are scarce or absent, not wholly exclusive to semen [Gam20, Sak20]), and semenogelin (Sg; seminal vesicles, target of RSID-Semen, detected in post-ejaculatory urine [Gam20, Rod19]). The cellular fraction contains spermatozoa; microscopy after cytological staining (Christmas tree, haematoxylin and eosin) provides direct evidence of spermatozoa [Gam20, Sak20]. (B) Illustrative scenarios: soluble markers may be detected when spermatozoa are absent or not recovered (vasectomy, azoospermia, condom use, oligospermia); in washing, degradation, or recovery loss, spermatozoa may not be seen while seminal plasma markers may still be detected [Gam20]. A positive protein assay indicates a marker, not sperm-cell presence [Gam20, Sak20]. Source: Original illustration based on [Gam20, Sak20, Rod19].
Interactive candidate: yes (case scenarios illustrating dissociation between soluble seminal plasma markers and microscopic sperm cell recovery).

## Figure 1.4 — Fig01-04_body-fluid-marker-matrix
Anchor: Section 1.6, after "…proteins, or spectral patterns, with differing levels of development and validation [Sij21]."
Caption: **Figure 1.4. Matrix of conventional and emerging diagnostic markers across forensically relevant body fluids.** Cross-comparison of seven body fluids (blood, semen, saliva, vaginal fluid, urine, sweat, faeces) across analytical modalities: catalytic/enzymatic tests, protein/lateral-flow assays, cytological microscopy, mRNA expression profiling, miRNA panels, DNA methylation, microbiome analysis, and mass-spectrometry proteomics. Symbols indicate operational status: target-specific assay supporting a defined marker (●), presumptive test with limited specificity (▲), research or developmental assay requiring casework validation (◆), no conventional test with broad validation (✕), and blank / not discussed (—). Columns for mRNA profiling, miRNA panels, DNA methylation, and microbiome analysis represent modalities investigated across several fluids but not presented as fluid-specific in the chapter text [Sij21, For16, Swa25, Sak20]. Footnote: Raman and FTIR spectroscopy have also been investigated to classify stains from molecular spectra (Section 1.6) [Sij21]. Source: Original illustration based on [Sij21, Sak20, Sal20, For16, Cut24, Gam20, Wor18, Sta11, Ste12, Swa25].
