# Chapter 10 Forensic Investigative Genetic Genealogy (FIGG)

## 10.1 Generating genealogy-compatible SNP data from forensic samples

Forensic investigative genetic genealogy (FIGG) requires a different data product from routine short tandem repeat (STR) profiling (Chapter 2). Rather than a compact standardized profile, it relies on dense, broadly distributed single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotypes to support comparisons across genealogy datasets [Kli21]. Butler’s chapter provides a general overview of SNPs and their applications [But12]. The challenge is to recover enough reliable, appropriately distributed genotypes from evidence that may be scarce, damaged, or contaminated with non-human DNA.

SNP microarrays assay a large, predefined set of loci efficiently, but depend on DNA molecules being suitable for hybridization. In an array workflow, decreasing input quantity and increasing fragmentation reduced call rates and genotype accuracy; effects on kinship classification also varied by relationship degree [Vri21]. These results illustrate why array performance cannot be reduced to a universal minimum-input threshold. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS), by contrast, sequences library-prepared DNA fragments broadly and can recover many SNPs without designing a separate assay for each locus. It offers flexibility for fragmented material, but requires sequencing, bioinformatic processing, and careful variant calling [Kli21]. Targeted massively parallel sequencing (MPS) assays selected loci, while hybridization capture enriches library fragments before sequencing. A targeted MPS assay yielded calls in some compromised postmortem samples, including cases lacking STR profiles, but not all [Til18]. Capture yielded low-coverage kinship SNP data from historical skeletal samples, with sample-dependent recovery [Gor20]. Kintelligence and the FORCE capture panel produced high call rates in one severely degraded bone, but showed workflow-specific differences at low input [Wat25]. Performance is workflow- and sample-specific, not universal [Til18, Gor20, Wat25]. A Swedish case report illustrates that WGS and repeated sequencing and imputation efforts were used to establish data from low-quantity, poor-quality evidence, but does not set general performance limits [Til21].

Before downstream comparison, genotype calls require quality assessment. Useful checks include call rate, concordance or discordance where reference genotypes exist, and—when sequencing—read support and allelic balance. In the array study, call rate tracked genotype error and classification performance, but was not interchangeable with either; its proposed indicators and observed limits apply to that array, sample set, and analytical method [Vri21]. Missing or erroneous calls can disrupt later comparisons, so a nominal marker count alone does not establish that a profile is reliable [Kli21, Vri21].

Different arrays and sequencing workflows do not necessarily assay the same SNPs. Selecting shared markers can improve comparability, while imputation can estimate untyped or missing genotypes from observed data and reference panels; accuracy depends on the amount and quality of observed data and the suitability of the reference panel, and imputation does not erase uncertainty [Kli21]. The resulting calls must then be harmonized and represented in the receiving platform’s required genotype-file format. Kli et al. describe selecting overlapping array loci from WGS data for a case profile, not a universal conversion procedure [Kli21]. Database search and IBD matching are covered in Section 10.2; family-tree development is covered in Section 10.3.

## 10.2 Database upload, IBD matching and relationship estimation

Once the genealogy-compatible SNP profile is ready, it can be submitted to a participating genealogy database and compared with profiles whose users are available for law-enforcement matching [Kli21, Gue21]. Familial searching in a national STR database is addressed separately in Chapter 5, Section 5.5. Platform-specific upload formats and marker overlap determine whether a profile can be accepted and which loci contribute to comparison; compatibility does not mean every database evaluates the same data in the same way [Kli21]. GEDmatch and FamilyTreeDNA (FTDNA) have both been used for such searches, but their participation rules and user privacy settings have differed, including opt-in or opt-out arrangements that determine which profiles are searchable [Kli21, Gue21]. Platforms also use different, sometimes proprietary, segment-calling procedures and thresholds for minimum segment length or marker count. Consequently, a profile may yield different match lists across platforms, and a relative below a platform’s threshold may not appear as a match [Kli21].

The central signal is identity by descent (IBD): genomic segments shared because they were inherited from a common ancestor. Databases summarize the lengths of detected shared segments using centimorgans (cM), a genetic-map measure of recombination distance; total shared cM aggregates segment lengths, while individual segment lengths provide additional information about the pattern of sharing [Kli21, Huf11]. Longer or more numerous segments generally support closer shared ancestry, and methods such as ERSA use both segment count and length to estimate the degree of recent relatedness [Huf11]. However, these measures do not identify a unique pedigree. Inheritance and recombination are stochastic, and the distributions of shared DNA overlap across relationship classes; distinct pedigrees can have similar expected sharing, while some genealogical relatives—especially distant ones—inherit no detectable IBD segment in common [Huf11, Kli19]. Database reports therefore provide an estimated range of relationships, not a definitive family relationship [Kli21, Gue21].

Detection and estimation also depend on the data and the model. Genotyping errors or missing calls can break true segments or create misleading ones; imputation may increase marker overlap but adds inferred genotypes whose reliability depends on the observed data and reference panel [Kli21]. Population composition matters both because a search can match only relatives represented in the database and because population history and background relatedness affect segment patterns [Huf11]. Erlich et al. found unequal match availability in a database whose participants were unevenly represented by ancestry; those findings describe that database, not an inherent difference in people’s capacity to match [Erl18]. Finally, relationship estimates vary with the segment caller, thresholds, genetic map, allele-frequency assumptions, and classification method; comparative analyses show trade-offs rather than a universally superior method [Huf11, Kli19]. General kinship and likelihood-ratio theory is covered in Chapter 4, Section 4.5.

## 10.3 Family-tree construction and investigative lead generation

The genealogical task turns a ranked list of IBD matches into plausible family branches. Researchers examine which matches also match one another, using shared-match patterns to form clusters that may point toward different ancestral lines. A cluster organizes the evidence but does not, by itself, establish a pedigree: proposed relationships must also fit the pattern across matches [Kli21, Gre19].

Researchers identify the people behind the match profiles and build or extend their family trees backward, generation by generation, toward possible common ancestors. Documentary sources may include civil and church records, censuses, newspapers, archives, obituaries, and existing family trees; each source must be assessed for reliability rather than accepted at face value [Kli21, Gre19, Kat20]. Once candidate ancestral couples or branches emerge, descendancy research traces their descendants forward. The aim is to find intersections among lines associated with separate match clusters and identify a family or branch consistent with the genetic and documentary evidence—not to infer an individual’s identity from a match label alone [Kli21, Gre19].

This work is iterative. A proposed tree should account for the matches as a group, while gaps or contradictions may prompt researchers to revisit an assumed relationship, seek additional records, or pursue another match. Ertürk and colleagues model the process as a sequence of choices: which match to investigate, which potential ancestral lines to extend, and when further work is no longer worthwhile. Their model highlights the trade-off between the chance of finding a useful intersection and the expected size of the tree; it is a decision framework, not a guarantee of real-world speed or success [Ert22]. The literature describes genealogical research as demanding substantial time and specialist skill in records and genetic genealogy, especially when matches are distant or difficult to identify [Kli21, Gre19].

Documented and biological histories may diverge. Missing, inaccessible, or erroneous records can leave branches unresolved, and records are unevenly available across families and populations [Kli21, Gre19, Kat20]. Adoption, unknown parentage, or misattributed parentage can make an apparently coherent paper pedigree biologically incomplete; endogamy and repeated intermarriage can also make shared DNA harder to reconcile with a simple branching tree [Kli21, Gre19]. These possibilities are reasons to treat a tree as a revisable hypothesis, not to force every match into the first plausible account.

Where a branch remains uncertain, targeted testing of volunteers or relatives associated with the implicated branch or region may help assess whether that branch fits the expected genetic pattern and narrow the search; it does not identify the unknown contributor [Kli21, Til21, Gre19]. The resulting lead may concern a family or branch and still leave several people plausible. Direct STR comparison of a candidate’s profile with the evidentiary profile is the separate confirmation step addressed in Section 10.4 [Kli21, Gre19].

## 10.4 Operational applications, landmark cases and direct STR confirmation

FIGG is an investigative route used when routine forensic work and other leads have not identified the unknown DNA contributor. Its role is to narrow an investigation toward a family or candidate; the genealogy result itself is not an identification. A candidate must still be assessed through ordinary investigative work and, in a suspect case, tested by direct comparison of that person’s STR profile with the evidentiary profile [Gue21].

The Golden State Killer investigation brought this distinction into public view. After years without a resolution, investigators used a distant relative’s database match to develop family-tree leads and focus on Joseph James DeAngelo; a later DNA sample collected from an item he discarded matched the crime-scene DNA [Gue18]. The case illustrates FIGG’s value as a way to reduce a broad field of possibilities, not as a genetic identification in its own right. Phillips cautions that public accounts did not disclose every technical step and that parts of the reported workflow were reconstructed from limited information [Phi18].

The Swedish double-murder investigation shows the same separation between lead and confirmation. Genealogical research, supported by repeated efforts to obtain usable SNP data and follow family branches, narrowed the search to a family branch involving brothers. Police then obtained reference samples from the candidates and compared their STR profiles directly with crime-scene DNA; the matching brother was later convicted [Til21]. The family-tree work identified candidates for testing, while the STR comparison established which tested brother’s DNA corresponded to the evidentiary profile.

Reported applications extend beyond these landmark investigations. Casework accounts describe FIGG use in cold cases, including unsolved homicides and sexual assaults, and to identify unidentified human remains [Gre19, Dow22]. For remains, genealogical research can help generate an identity hypothesis that is then assessed against available reference material and other evidence [Gre24]. These published case profiles show that the method has been applied to varied investigative problems, but they are not a complete or unbiased tally of success. Cases may remain unresolved, be handled privately, or go unreported, and public compilations lack a consistent denominator of all attempts; reported outcomes therefore cannot establish an overall success rate or a representative pattern of who benefits [Dow22, Gre24].

A direct STR match supports correspondence between the candidate’s tested DNA profile and the DNA profile recovered as evidence. It does not, by itself, prove guilt or establish when, where, or through what activity the DNA was deposited; those questions require the wider evidence and investigation [Til21, Gue21].

## 10.5 Relational genetic privacy, consent and population identifiability

A genealogy database search can use one participant’s genotype to produce information about people who never submitted DNA. A relative’s match may provide a route into family records and toward a non-participant, who can become identifiable or attract investigative scrutiny without having chosen to enter a genealogy service [Ram18, Scu19b]. The privacy interest is therefore relational: consent to use one person’s data does not amount to consent from the relatives whose connections help make that data informative.

The scale of this exposure depends on database coverage, ancestry composition, and the records available to connect genetic relatives to named people. Erlich and colleagues observed frequent distant-relative matches in a large consumer-genomics dataset, then used a population model to project how match availability might change as database coverage grew [Erl18]. Those projections assumed random sampling and simplified away population structure and inbreeding; they are conditional estimates, not universal probabilities of identification. The study also distinguished obtaining a relative match from narrowing the identity of the target: its latter estimates depended on demographic clues and genealogical records, not genetic matching alone [Erl18]. The underlying dataset was weighted toward people of North European ancestry, and match availability differed across ancestry groups. Treating its aggregate results or European-ancestry projections as representative of every population would therefore overstate what the evidence establishes [Erl18]. Unequal access to family-history records can further shape whether a match develops into an identifiable branch, so population identifiability reflects both genetic representation and documentary visibility [Kat20]. A proof-of-principle trace from a public research genome through distant relatives demonstrates that re-identification can occur, but does not establish how often it succeeds for untested people generally [Erl18].

Privacy risks also arise from the technical information returned by upload-enabled services. Edge and Coop showed how repeated comparisons can accumulate information from shared-segment locations, and described engineered uploads that could probe or infer genotypes under certain segment-calling and reporting conditions [Edg19]. Their analyses used public genotype data and an artificial-kit demonstration; they did not extract real customers’ genomes. The feasibility and extent of such attacks therefore depend on platform design and disclosure, rather than following automatically from any database match [Edg19].

The familial effects can extend beyond identification: a search may expose unexpected biological relationships, draw uninvolved relatives into questioning, or create records about people later excluded from suspicion. Such effects may reach across borders and persist beyond the participant’s own interaction with the service [Kat20, Scu19b]. A participant’s informed choice may authorize use of that participant’s data under a service’s terms, but cannot settle the privacy interests or social consequences for relatives who did not participate [Ram18, Scu19b].

## 10.6 Policy, platform rules and program governance

The 2019 U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) Interim Policy, as described in the literature, covered DOJ-funded agencies and cases in which DOJ participated in the genealogy stage. It limited FIGG to violent crimes and unidentified remains, after reasonable investigative leads and a CODIS (Combined DNA Index System) search produced no probative, confirmed match. A genealogy association alone could not justify arrest; direct STR comparison was required before arrest [Gue21]. The policy was interim, not a nationwide statute, and its reported direction to remove FIGG data after confirmation was not a universal retention rule [Kat20].

The 2023 guidance from NTVIC (the National Technology Validation and Implementation Collaborative) likewise recommends reasonable leads first and asks jurisdictions to define eligibility, including crime categories, unidentified remains, and post-conviction testing. The 2025 update adds an explicit necessity-and-proportionality test, while both the 2023 and 2025 guidelines place the program under a designated FIGG Responsible Authority (FIGG RA) [Wic23, Wic25]. It allows case-by-case exceptions for degraded or low-input evidence, an unavailable or uninformative CODIS search, or an exigent public-safety risk [Wic23, Wic25]. Both are adaptable recommendations, not binding law everywhere; statutes, court orders, and local policy govern.

A genealogy association remains a lead: the 2025 guidance recommends direct STR confirmation when proceedings will be initiated and an STR profile is available [Wic25].

Data handling should be differentiated. The 2025 recommendations call for long-term retention of crime-scene SNP profiles and reproducible case records, but prompt destruction of excluded persons’ samples after case closure, subject to law [Wic25]. Jurisdictional rules govern third-party samples; vendors should not retain case materials beyond those rules unless an agreement with the authority provides otherwise. Case files should preserve approvals, analytical files, and pipeline versions for review and disclosure [Wic25].

Provider rules add a changeable layer. In provider terms examined in the literature, GEDmatch used opt-in law-enforcement matching for defined violent crimes and unidentified remains, while FamilyTreeDNA used a dedicated service with an opt-out setting; opting out did not prevent disclosure under valid legal process [Ske20]. Later GEDmatch terms changes and ambiguities underscore that these are dated platform mechanisms, not stable legal standards [Kli21]. NTVIC recommends using only services that permit law-enforcement searches and honoring user settings [Wic25].

NTVIC keeps responsibility with the investigating authority, even when work is outsourced. It recommends approving laboratories and genealogists, accreditation and quality systems, competency assessment, validated and documented tools, and independent technical review. Jurisdictions may also establish oversight panels, complaint routes, and program reporting; these are recommendations, not uniform requirements [Wic23, Wic25]. Platform permission is not legal authorization, and a user’s setting is not consent on behalf of affected relatives [Scu19b, Ske20].

## 10.7 Public perspectives, legitimacy and accountability

Public-opinion studies suggest that support for FIGG depends on purpose. An early U.S. online survey found greater support for police access to genealogy data in violent-crime than nonviolent investigations, but its Amazon Mechanical Turk sample was not designed to represent the U.S. population and followed the highly publicized Golden State Killer arrest [Gue18]. A later U.S. online-panel survey, stratified on demographics but not probability-sampled, again found stronger support for violent-crime uses than nonviolent offenses, and less support for searches of hypothetical medical-research databases than genealogy databases [Gue25]. It covered more purposes, but asked binary allow-or-not judgments rather than measuring understanding, perceived trade-offs, or support for safeguards. Its design captures stated attitudes, not a population-wide mandate.

Focus groups make conditional support more visible. Across focus groups in U.S. cities, participants generally favored FIGG in cold cases and violent crimes, while views on active investigations and nonviolent offenses diverged. They supported clear notice that a database permits law-enforcement matching but disagreed on opt-in versus opt-out: many preferred opt-in while recognizing it could shrink the searchable pool. Participants also differed on post-match notification; some wanted to know about a match, while others feared it could draw relatives into proceedings or alert a suspect [Dah24]. The small, geographically limited qualitative sample, and use of a favorable case video, mean these findings illuminate reasons, not the prevalence of views nationwide.

Participants commonly called for trained or credentialed practitioners and some form of accountability, but did not settle on who should conduct searches or what oversight should entail [Dah24]. Public-attitude evidence can inform those choices, but cannot stand in for individual consent, legal authority, or independent oversight.

A modified policy Delphi captures expert, not public, priorities. Experts flagged patchwork governance, laboratory quality, and data and sample handling; most opposed disregarding database users’ consent selections or platform terms. The panel reached no consensus, and transparency about uses and outcomes did not meet the study’s threshold for a top-priority practice [Gue25b]. These priorities neither establish public endorsement nor demonstrate agreement on specific safeguards.

Legitimacy also depends on whether practice and outcomes can be scrutinized. A compilation of publicly confirmed successful cases documents applications and possible benefits, including exoneration, but omits undisclosed, ongoing, and unsuccessful investigations, so cannot provide a denominator for success rates [Dow22]. Reporting should make eligibility and selection criteria visible and distinguish investigative leads, confirmed identities, and final case outcomes. It should also allow scrutiny of who benefits: published profiles suggest uneven representation, but missing data and selection through public reporting prevent firm conclusions about equitable access [Dow22].

## Chapter summary

FIGG links the recovery of genealogy-compatible SNP data to database matching and family-tree research, producing investigative leads that may narrow attention to a family or candidate. Its technical results depend on sample quality, platform and model choices, database composition, and documentary records; its use also raises relational privacy, governance, and legitimacy questions. A genealogy match or family-tree hypothesis is not an identification: this chapter treats direct STR comparison as the step that confirms DNA-source correspondence, while guilt and the activity that deposited DNA remain separate questions.
