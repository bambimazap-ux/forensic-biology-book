# Chapter 11 Fingerprints: Fundamentals and Development

## 11.1 Friction-ridge skin: formation, pattern, and anatomy

Friction-ridge skin covers the volar surfaces of the fingers, palms, toes, and soles; on fingertips, its raised ridges form familiar fingerprint patterns [Mac09, Glo23]. These patterns arise prenatally. Volar pads—temporary swellings of developing digits—are present before ridge formation and become less prominent as the hand grows. Their changing shape and position provide part of the local developmental setting, but are not a simple template that dictates a pattern [Glo23, Wer02, Kuc05]. Primary ridges spread across the digits, with timing that differs by digit and by the developmental event being described [Glo23, Wer02]. The historical contributions of Galton, Faulds, and Henry are covered in Chapter 0.

An influential mechanical model proposed that growth and constraints compress the basal epidermis, causing it to buckle into ridges; it linked the resulting stresses partly to digit growth and volar-pad regression [Kuc05]. This is a proposed mechanism, not an established account. Developmental evidence also implicates epithelial signaling: EDAR and WNT promote ridge-associated growth, while BMP signaling can oppose WNT activity. Glover and colleagues propose that these interactions generate periodic ridges and that patterning advances in waves from variable initiation sites shaped by digit anatomy [Glo23]. Their mechanistic tests rely substantially on mouse tissue and cultured or organoid material, so direct causal evidence in human fetal development remains limited. Human genetic findings also implicate limb-development pathways, suggesting that digit growth can influence pattern outcomes without those genes acting directly in ridge-forming epithelium [Li22].

Arches have no triradius, loops have one, and whorls have two [Li22]. Glover and colleagues model different ridge-field patterns through variation in the timing, location, and angle of wave initiation; collision of three waves forms a triradius [Glo23].

A ridge is a raised band of the skin surface, with a furrow between neighboring ridges. Beneath the surface, primary epidermal ridges project toward the dermis, while alternating structures correspond to surface furrows; the epidermis and dermis are joined at a folded interface [Wer02]. Eccrine sweat glands develop from primary ridges. Their ducts pass through the epidermis and open at the surface as pores, commonly along ridge apices [Glo23, Wer02]. A ridge may end or divide into branches; these local configurations are called minutiae. Here they are anatomical descriptions only, not a framework for evaluating a comparison. Residue composition is addressed in Section 11.2 and development methods in Sections 11.3–11.5.

Permanence concerns the stability of features on the skin across observations; persistence concerns whether impressions remain sufficiently similar across time and capture conditions for their ridge arrangement to remain recognizable despite differences caused by physiology, pressure, substrate, or image capture [Mon19]. The two are not interchangeable: broad ridge flow can remain recognizable while local ridge paths vary in appearance, and pores and ridge-edge shapes are less stable across observations and recording conditions [Mon19]. The formal detail framework and its use in fingerprint comparison are reserved for Chapter 12.

## 11.2 Latent fingermark residue as a variable chemical trace

Unlike the skin structures described in Section 11.1, a latent fingermark is transferred material whose composition reflects secretion, contact history, deposition, and subsequent change. It is therefore misleading to treat every mark as a fixed sample of “sweat”: deposits can combine an aqueous eccrine contribution with lipids and other material acquired from the skin or environment [Bec26, Rob22].

Eccrine secretion contributes water and dissolved inorganic and organic compounds, including salts and amino acids. Alanine, glycine, serine, leucine, and lysine recur in studies of fingermark constituents, but their detection does not imply a stable amount or full set in every mark [Rob22]. Lipid-rich sebaceous material can also be transferred, often indirectly through contact with sebaceous areas of the face or hair. Reported constituents include squalene, fatty acids, and cholesterol [Rob22]. In one study of natural and deliberately groomed deposits, touching sebum-rich facial skin before deposition increased fatty acids and squalene, while amino-acid quantities did not show the same change [Cro10]. This illustrates a route by which recent contact alters the trace, not a universal difference between all natural and groomed marks.

Urea and uric acid have each been reported in an isolated study [Rob22]. Other detected material includes peptide- or protein-related material and compounds acquired from cosmetics, medications, or other contacts; some findings therefore reflect exogenous material rather than secretion alone [Bec26, Rob22]. Donor-related factors such as health, diet, smoking, medication, and skin-product use may contribute, but are not consistently measured across studies [Rob22]. The quantity and balance of constituents also depend on deposition: prior washing, rubbing or grooming, recent contact, contact duration and pressure, and repeated touches change what is available at the fingertip and what reaches the surface [Rob22]. Studies based on cleaned hands and controlled deposits therefore do not necessarily describe marks left during ordinary activity.

Once deposited, material interacts with the substrate. Surface roughness and wettability affect transfer and retention, while sampling surfaces can themselves contribute signals—for example, paper may complicate amino-acid analysis. The recovered chemical profile is not simply the original fingertip mixture; it reflects both deposit and surface, as well as what the analytical method can extract or detect [Bec26, Rob22]. Exposure to light and heat, along with elapsed time, can alter residue. Reported studies examine lipid degradation, oxidation, and changes in deposited material, but do not establish a uniform trajectory applicable across marks and surfaces [Bec26, Rob22].

The evidence supports broad compositional variation, not a canonical chemical profile for an individual mark. Robson and colleagues’ systematic review found substantial differences among studies in donor numbers and characteristics, collection and grooming protocols, substrates, storage, analytes, and analytical procedures; many studies reported a constituent’s presence rather than comparable amounts [Rob22]. Different methods also provide different levels of chemical specificity, so failure to report a compound is not proof that it was absent. Bécue likewise notes that small or idealized samples and the limited use of realistic casework conditions restrict generalization [Bec26]. Residue composition therefore guides the choice of development approach, but published profiles cannot be read as a prediction of the composition of a particular deposit [Bec26, Rob22].

## 11.3 Porous surfaces: amino-acid reagents and physical developer

Paper and similar porous materials take up deposited liquid into a network of fibers and spaces, rather than leaving all residue on an exposed surface. This can carry trace material below the surface, where it is less accessible to methods that act only on surface deposits. Amino acids retained in paper bind strongly to cellulose and, under ordinary dry conditions, show little migration; this helps preserve the ridge pattern for chemical development [Jel09b]. Paper is not uniform: absorbency, fiber composition, surface treatment, and additives vary, so the response of one paper stock cannot be assumed for another [Bec20].

Ninhydrin, 1,8-diazafluoren-9-one (DFO), and 1,2-indanedione (IND) exploit amino acids retained in porous materials. Ninhydrin reacts with amino acids through a Strecker-type degradation (decarboxylation), ultimately forming Ruhemann’s purple, a strongly colored product that reveals ridge detail under visible light [Jel09b]. The purple product is not intrinsically fluorescent, although a zinc-salt treatment can alter its color and support luminescent viewing. Ninhydrin therefore offers a readily visible signal, but heating can also increase background staining when the reagent reacts with paper constituents [Jel09b].

DFO also reacts with amino-acid components, through an imine intermediate and subsequent reaction steps that yield a red product. Its visible color is relatively pale, while its strong luminescence can make developed marks easier to detect under suitable illumination and filters [Jel09b]. IND reacts with α-amino acids to produce a pale-pink color and intense luminescence. Zinc chloride can be included in the formulation or applied to increase luminescence and deepen the visible color; the proposed structure of the final IND reaction product, however, has not been conclusively established [Jel09b]. Comparisons of DFO and IND have not produced a context-free ranking: results vary with formulation, substrate, and development conditions [Jel09b, Bec22].

Physical developer (PD) is a distinct, silver-based process rather than another amino-acid reagent. In its redox system, iron(II) reduces silver ions to finely divided metallic silver, which deposits on fatty-acid and lipid components and makes the developed pattern appear dark [Sod16]. Because these less water-soluble components may remain when water exposure has removed more soluble material, PD can be useful for selected wet porous items; reports also describe its value on aged documents [Bec20, Sod16]. Paper fillers and binders can interfere with development, and some papers may require an acid pre-treatment [Sod16].

Formulation and item history shape what these methods reveal. Some formulations (for example excess methanol) can cause ink to run [Bec20]. Reagent interactions with paper or thermal coatings can also darken backgrounds or damage printed information [Jel09b, Bec20, Bec22]. Aging and storage conditions also affect performance, without establishing a single age-based rule for all papers [Bec26, Bec22]. Marriott and colleagues compared IND-Zn → ninhydrin → PD with DFO → ninhydrin → PD on several paper types and older examination booklets. Their pseudo-operational (using older booklets expected to have been naturally handled, not controlled donor deposits) results favored the IND-Zn sequence, but controlled donor trials showed little overall difference between the complete sequences; PD also performed poorly on most of the tested paper stocks [Mar14f]. Such sequences are evidence to weigh against the specific substrate, inks, condition, and prior treatment—not a universal recipe.

## 11.4 Nonporous, wet, and special surfaces: physical, chemical, and optical development

On nonporous materials, transferred residue remains largely at the exposed interface, allowing physical powders to attach to the mark and create visible contrast. Powder development remains an established approach, but the volume of research on new formulations should not be mistaken for proven improvement: reviews caution that many nanoparticle studies are preliminary or of limited forensic relevance, and that dry nanoparticle application may raise health-and-safety concerns [Bec26, Bec20, Bec22].

Cyanoacrylate (CA) fuming exposes an item to ethyl cyanoacrylate vapor, which polymerizes along residue-bearing ridges and leaves a visible deposit [War07]. Wargacki and colleagues’ model experiments support anionic chain polymerization initiated chiefly by carboxylate groups, including lactate and the carboxylate functionality of amino acids. Water alone produced short products rather than the longer chains associated with developed marks; acidic conditions favored chain termination, while more basic conditions allowed greater chain growth. This offers a chemical account of development, but the experiments used model residues and do not establish a complete mechanism for every natural mark [War07].

The CA deposit may be hard to distinguish from the background, so fluorescent dye stains such as Basic Yellow 40 or Rhodamine 6G can be used to improve contrast [Bec20, Bec22]. Appropriate excitation sources and viewing filters make the dye’s emission visible while suppressing background. Their benefit varies with substrate and viewing conditions, so fluorescence is an observation mode rather than a guarantee of improved ridge detail [Bec26, Bec20]. Casework evidence for complementarity between preliminary optical examination and physicochemical processing is summarized in Section 11.5 [Bec26].

For difficult substrates, forensic vacuum metal deposition (fVMD) is a specialist option with reported use on materials including plastics, textiles, and polymer banknotes, as well as aged or otherwise challenging impressions [Dov25]. In the common gold–zinc process, gold provides a surface for subsequent zinc deposition, but the detailed mechanism that produces contrast remains unsettled. Proposed explanations involving condensation, absorption into residue, and metal-cluster morphology each leave observations unexplained; the review finds none conclusively established [Dov25].

Small-particle reagent (SPR) addresses wet or moist nonporous items. Fine particles suspended in a liquid adhere to water-insoluble oily or fatty residue, producing a visible deposit on surfaces such as glass, plastic, and metal [Bum16]. Fluorescent variants can improve detection of weak marks or contrast against multicolored backgrounds, although formulations and results vary across substrates [Bum16]. SPR has also been applied to adhesive surfaces, including the adhesive side of tape [Bum16]. For powder suspensions on tape, reviews describe formulation-specific methods rather than a single validated method for all tape types [Bec26, Bec22].

New materials and analytical imaging—including chemical imaging of fingermark traces—may reveal information beyond ridge contrast, but the literature remains developing. Reviews describe many such studies as pilot work on limited or idealized samples; some imaging approaches also carry practical limits such as slow acquisition [Bec26]. They are research tools, not routine replacements for established powder, CA, or other substrate-directed processes [Bec26, Bec20, Bec22, Ble20].

## 11.5 Process selection, sequencing, imaging, and documentation

No single development sequence suits every item. Selection depends on how the surface retains residue, its present condition, the mark’s likely composition and history, and whether earlier treatment or another examination may change what remains available. Sears and colleagues frame these interactions among mark, surface, and environment as central to evaluating a process [Sea12]. Wetting, aging, heat, contamination, and prior processing can alter both the residue and the substrate; the same treatment may therefore succeed on one item and fail on another [Bec26, Bec20, Bec22].

Before treatment, items should be examined under ordinary and suitable forensic illumination, with filters where useful, because optical examination may reveal marks that processing does not. In a casework review of vehicle and scene marks, preliminary UV-A, visible-light, and laser examination located many marks not found by subsequent physicochemical processing, while the two approaches detected largely different sets [Bec26]. For thermal paper, a collaborative exercise likewise recommended examination with a forensic light source and filters before chemical treatment; processing could alter or remove printed information, making prior recording important [Bec22]. These examples support an initial non-destructive examination, not a claim that one illumination setup will reveal every mark.

Sequences can add complementary recovery, but their value is item- and study-specific. The Marriott study’s contrast between controlled donor trials and pseudo-operational booklet results is summarized in Section 11.3 [Mar14f]. In other settings, the reviews describe optical examination, chemical development, and later enhancement as complementary, while also noting that some processes can impair later development or other evidence [Bec26, Bec20, Bec22]. Sequence choices should account for the information to be preserved, including visible detail, printed text, and potential downstream DNA or chemical examination [Bec26, Bec22].

A useful record begins with the item as received: its condition, surface and apparent wetting or damage, the location and orientation of visible or suspected marks, and any relevant handling or prior processing. Photograph the item and visible detail before treatment, then capture consequential stages under the illumination and filters that best show the mark. Record those viewing conditions alongside the treatment order and process conditions, so later examiners can distinguish changes caused by processing from differences in observation. The papers do not establish one universal casework photography protocol; Marriott et al., for example, report digital imaging after each sequence step and specify the illumination and filters used, while the broader reviews stress recording processing conditions and preserving information that treatment may alter [Bec26, Bec22, Mar14f].

Performance claims should be read in light of how the evidence was produced. Sears et al. distinguish early screening with model materials from planted-mark trials, pseudo-operational studies, and full operational trials; each can answer different questions, but early-stage results alone do not establish casework performance [Sea12]. The reviews similarly caution that small donor sets, enriched or artificial marks, pristine substrates, and limited condition ranges can make results hard to transfer to operational items [Bec26, Bec20, Bec22]. Realistic trials, clearly reported item and process conditions, and comparisons that test each step’s added contribution provide a stronger basis for choosing a sequence than an isolated high-yield demonstration [Bec26, Mar14f, Sea12].

## Chapter summary

Friction-ridge patterns arise through prenatal development, while the latent material transferred in a fingermark varies with secretion, contact, deposition, substrate, and change after deposition. Development methods target different properties of that variable residue, so their performance depends on the surface and item history; emerging imaging approaches remain research tools rather than routine substitutes for established processes [Glo23, Mon19, Bec26, Rob22].

Sound processing therefore relies on substrate-specific choices, non-destructive examination where useful, and records of the item, illumination, and treatment stages. These recovery methods do not themselves provide a comparison or source conclusion: the formal Level 1/2/3 framework and fingerprint comparison belong to Chapter 12.
