# Chapter 2 Profile Characterization

## 2.1 Evidence Sampling and Contamination Control

DNA collection is shaped by the trace, the surface, and the collection tool. "Touch DNA" refers to low-level biological material recovered from an object after contact; in such samples, the amount available for analysis may be limited. Sampling performance depends not only on how much material is lifted from a surface but also on how much is released from the collection device during extraction. These are distinct stages: extraction efficiency concerns release from the swab into the extraction solution, whereas recovery efficiency reflects the combined transfer from the surface through to that solution [Bru24b]. Surface texture and porosity, swab-tip structure and material, wetting solution, collection technique, and downstream extraction can all affect the result.

No single collection procedure is best for every surface and trace. Wetting can aid collection of dried material, while a wet-dry sequence is used in some workflows; their performance depends on the sample, substrate, and extraction that follows [Bru24b, Con25]. A systematic review of touch-DNA studies found that single-swabbing performed well across several outcome groupings, but the studies differed in substrates, protocols, and measures of success. Its findings do not establish a universal ranking of single- and double-swabbing [Toz22]. Tape can remove loose fibres from fabric, so swabbing may be preferable for fabrics with loose fibres [Bru24b]. Tape lifting involves a more complex subsequent extraction and showed low performance on human skin in the comparison reviewed [Toz22]. Cutting or punching is described for removable paper and textiles; paper cuttings often recover too poorly to yield a DNA profile [Bru24b].

Comparisons of swab materials are likewise conditional. A preferred swab may differ by DNA source, substrate, product design, and extraction chemistry; even products made from the same broad material need not perform alike. Studies also use different outcomes, from measured DNA quantity to profile success, and these are not interchangeable. The evidence therefore supports validating a material-substrate-workflow combination rather than adopting a universal material ranking [Bru24b, Con25].

A Swiss investigation found that most identified contaminations were associated with police collection, while indirect transfer also occurred in laboratory settings [Bas18]. A broader contamination-minimization program can combine environmental monitoring with checks of risk surfaces, procedure compliance, staff proficiency, and case-specific root-cause analysis [Oor15]. Elimination databases compare evidence profiles with profiles of personnel or others who may legitimately encounter exhibits; they can support investigation after a possible contaminant is detected, but do not replace preventive practice. Their coverage and governance vary among systems [Nog25].

## 2.2 DNA Extraction, Purification, and Challenging Samples

Extraction lyses cells or tissue, separates DNA from the substrate and other components, and purifies it for analysis. Material may be lost or inhibitors retained at each stage; sample type, substrate, condition, and DNA scarcity shape method choice, so no chemistry suits every combination [Lee17]. Organic extraction, Chelex, silica-based purification, magnetic beads, and automated systems trade off yield, inhibitor removal, handling, throughput, and sample loss [Lee17]. In a pilot study limited to bloodstains on several surfaces, PCIA produced more DNA than Chelex, but the study measured yield rather than STR profile success and does not establish a general ranking across sample types [Dub20]. In hard-tissue studies, measured DNA yield and STR profiling success did not necessarily move together; a meta-analysis associated solid-phase methods with higher profiling success even though some liquid-phase methods produced more DNA [Fin22].

PCR inhibitors carried into an extract can interfere with amplification even when DNA template is present; they may come from biological material, the substrate, or the environment and affect different reaction components [Ala12]. Purification, dilution, or altered amplification conditions can help, but may also reduce usable DNA or affect the assay, so require case- and method-specific evaluation [Ala12]. See Section 2.3 for qPCR indicators relevant to inhibition and degradation.

Differential extraction is used, particularly for sexual-assault evidence, to enrich sperm-cell DNA separately from non-sperm cellular material. The usual chemical process lyses non-sperm cells first, then lyses the more resistant sperm cells in a later step. It aims to produce fractions with different DNA compositions, not pure fractions or a complete separation [Cla21]. A collaborative study found substantial variation among laboratory protocols and outcomes, including loss of male DNA and instances where male DNA was recovered in the non-sperm fraction. The results support treating differential extraction as a protocol-dependent enrichment step, not as a guarantee that each fraction contains DNA from only one contributor [Vui11].

Bone and teeth require preparation suited to their mineral matrix and often to environmental damage. Surface cleaning, grinding, and demineralization may be used before purification, but sampling is destructive and protocols vary [Lat18]. A systematic review and meta-analysis associated solid-phase methods and a demineralization pre-step with improved STR profiling success, although protocol variation and other confounders limit attribution [Fin22]. Shed hair is another challenging matrix: nuclear DNA can be present in hair shafts, but it is highly fragmented, and the cited study did not establish routine STR recovery from rootless shafts. Extraction approaches that retain shorter fragments may matter, but their value for STR profiling remains to be demonstrated [Bra18].

## 2.3 Quantification and Quality Assessment by Real-Time PCR

Real-time PCR (qPCR) assays can quantify total human DNA and, in multiplex formats, human male DNA. Total-human quantification helps set STR template input; a male-specific target can inform whether male DNA is present against a human-DNA background. Comparing estimates may guide consideration of autosomal or male-focused typing, but does not identify the DNA source or establish exact sample composition [Hud08, Ewi16].

Some systems also use short and longer human targets to assess degradation. Because fragmentation is more likely to disrupt a longer target, the relative amplification of longer and shorter targets provides an indirect degradation index. This is a measure defined by the assay's targets and conditions, not a direct measurement of fragment-length distribution. An internal PCR control (IPC), added to the reaction, can signal reduced amplification efficiency and help assess possible inhibition. Reading the degradation measure alongside the IPC can inform whether inhibition may contribute to a weak target signal; neither measure alone is a definitive diagnosis [Hud08, Ewi16].

Quantification results guide workflow decisions rather than guarantee downstream performance. They may inform template input, the choice between autosomal or male-specific testing, or whether further treatment of an extract should be considered. The meaning of a ratio or control shift depends on the assay and its laboratory validation. A relatively high DNA quantity does not ensure a complete, balanced STR profile, while a low estimate does not necessarily mean that no useful result can be obtained [Hud08, Ewi16].

## 2.4 STR Markers and Multiplex Amplification

At an STR locus, alleles can differ in the number and arrangement of repeated units. Some loci have simple repeat structures; others contain multiple repeat blocks or sequence variants. Routine length-based typing assigns alleles by the size of amplified fragments, but that label does not always capture the full sequence. Variants within repeat or flanking regions can produce fragments of the same length but different sequences, or alter amplification and allele designation [But06c, But12e].

Core marker sets such as CODIS and the European Standard Set (ESS) support comparison of profiles across laboratories and systems. Locus selection and kit design must balance discrimination with reliable amplification, compatibility within a multiplex, and practical fragment-size ranges. Core sets and commercial kits have changed over time, so comparison depends on shared loci and consistent allele nomenclature rather than an assumption that every kit contains the same panel [But06c, But12e].

Multiplex PCR amplifies multiple STR loci in one reaction using multiple primer pairs. The primers must function under shared conditions without excessive interactions or nonspecific products, and the resulting products must be distinguishable through their sizes and fluorescent labels. Developers balance primer concentrations and reaction conditions to support amplification across loci; adding targets makes this task more complex. Commercial multiplexes are optimized systems, but performance remains dependent on the kit, sample, and validated laboratory conditions [But12f, Mcd24]. Separation and detection of the resulting products are described in Section 2.5.

Amelogenin is a commonly used marker that distinguishes AMELX- and AMELY-derived products by their length difference. Detection of an AMELX product alone or of both products reports which target sequences amplified in that reaction; it is not an infallible determination of sex or sex-chromosome composition. Reported AMELY failures are most often attributed to deletions encompassing AMELY, while low-template stochastic allele drop-out is a separate way the expected product may be absent [But14]. Primer-binding-site variation has been proposed, but not confirmed, as an explanation for AMELY failure; reported AMELX primer-region variants may complicate mixture interpretation when relative amplicon amounts are used [But14].

## 2.5 Capillary Electrophoresis and Generation of the Electropherogram

After multiplex PCR, fluorescently labelled STR amplicons are introduced into a narrow capillary containing a polymer sieving matrix. An electric field moves the negatively charged DNA through the matrix; shorter fragments generally migrate through it more readily than longer fragments. Separation is primarily by fragment size, although sequence and run conditions can also affect migration. Migration time alone is therefore not an allele designation, and measured migration must be calibrated against reference materials [But12b, She12].

As fragments pass a detection window, laser-induced fluorescence is recorded. STR kits use several fluorescent dyes, allowing products with overlapping size ranges to be distinguished by both dye channel and migration. Because dye-emission spectra overlap, spectral calibration is used to estimate how each dye contributes to signals recorded across detector channels. The resulting calibration is specific to the instrument and analytical setup; incomplete correction can create apparent signal in another channel without a distinct amplicon there. Such spectral effects, including pull-up, are considered with other electropherogram features in Section 2.6 [But12b].

The internal size standard and allelic ladder have different roles. Known-size fragments in the internal standard provide a within-run reference for converting migration into estimated fragment sizes. The standard supports sizing but does not identify an STR allele. Allele designation instead compares sized sample fragments with the alleles represented in a ladder for the relevant loci [But12b, Mor01]. Sequence-dependent mobility can make apparent size alone an imperfect basis for naming an allele [But12b].

An electropherogram is a processed representation of detected fluorescence, not the raw detector record. Sample peaks represent fluorescent PCR products, whereas signals from the size standard and allelic ladder arise from reference fragments. Channel assignment, size estimates, peak detection, and allele labels are analytical outputs. Guidelines distinguish electropherogram data from software-derived peak descriptors and call for allele-designation criteria supported by laboratory validation [Sci21].

## 2.6 Reading the Electropherogram and Profile Quality

A detected peak is not automatically a true allele: background, amplification products, and instrument effects can produce signals in allele-sized positions [Sci21]. The analytical threshold (AT) is a laboratory-validated signal level used to distinguish peaks from background noise. It neither guarantees that every peak above it is allelic nor proves that no allele is present below it [Bre13b, Sci21].

The stochastic threshold addresses a different question: it is a laboratory-validated peak-height level below which a detected allele indicates increased risk that its heterozygous partner has dropped out. It is therefore a warning about profile completeness, not a signal-versus-noise boundary. Both thresholds depend on the laboratory's validated kit, instrument, amplification, and detection conditions; neither is universal [Sci21, Gil12]. Low-template validation illustrates why decision rules are tied to a particular workflow rather than transferable defaults [Car09].

Stutter is a minor PCR product, commonly one repeat unit shorter than its parent allele, consistent with strand slippage during amplification [Wal96]. Its proportion varies among loci [Lec04]; at vWA, stutter also varies with allele length and repeat structure, including the length of uninterrupted repeat tracts [Wal96]. Heterozygote balance describes the relative signal from the two alleles at a heterozygous locus. Observed balance varies across casework samples, so expectations should be grounded in validated data rather than a universal ratio [Lec04, Sci21].

Allele drop-out occurs when an allele present in the sample is not detected above the analytical threshold. Drop-in describes an additional allele signal not accounted for by the assumed profile. Low-template amplification increases stochastic risk of allele drop-out and imbalance; drop-in may arise from contamination [Gil12, Gil15, But12c]. A single peak pattern may not show whether an allele is absent from the sample or merely undetected in that reaction. These technical possibilities do not, by themselves, establish the number of contributors.

Pull-up and spectral calibration are described in Section 2.5. An off-scale peak has saturated the detector's linear range, so its displayed height does not reliably represent its true signal; saturation can also make associated stutter or pull-up more prominent [Sci21]. For degradation, see Section 2.3; profiles may show reduced signal or drop-out at longer amplicons while shorter products remain detectable [But12]. Partial profiles and weak peaks must therefore be assessed in light of signal level, artifacts, and fragment-size pattern, without treating any single feature as a definitive diagnosis. Contributor interpretation and statistical evaluation are addressed in Chapters 3 and 4.

## 2.7 STR Sequencing by MPS as an Emerging Option

Chapter 0, Section 0.6 introduced the additional sequence detail MPS can provide for STRs; routine use depends on shared rules for sequence alignment and allele designation. ISFG guidance distinguishes the observed sequence, its alignment to a reference, and the resulting nomenclature; differences in strand or repeat boundaries can otherwise yield inconsistent descriptions [Par16]. Sequence calls therefore need standardized designations and tools that can translate them into established CE-based nomenclature. This compatibility matters because many existing profiles are length-based and CE results will continue to be generated. Later recommendations address common reporting and automated designation while retaining links to CE data [Get23].

A reliable result depends on the sequencing platform, panel, and analysis pipeline. Validation must assess allele-calling accuracy, repeatability, reproducibility, sensitivity, and performance with challenging samples. Read coverage, thresholds, stutter handling, and software rules can affect whether a sequence is reported as supported, uncertain, or not called. The developmental validation of one forensic MPS system illustrates the scope of such assessment, but does not establish interchangeable performance across platforms or laboratories [Jag17]. Cross-laboratory comparison also depends on consistent sequence data, nomenclature, and analysis practices [Alo18].

MPS can add useful STR detail, but it brings data-management, bioinformatics, training, and validation demands. CE remains an established method with mature workflows and a large legacy collection of profiles; maintaining comparisons with length-based results is one reason sequencing complements rather than automatically replaces CE [Alo18, Bru18].

## Summary

Profile characterization turns collected material into STR measurements through linked stages of sampling, extraction, quantification, amplification, separation, and signal review. Each stage can affect the information that reaches the next, and method performance depends on the sample, the workflow, and laboratory validation rather than on any single yield or signal measure.

An electropherogram is an analytical measurement with limits: signal thresholds, artifacts, and sample condition constrain what can be read from it. These technical findings inform, but do not by themselves resolve, contributor interpretation or statistical evaluation in Chapters 3 and 4.
