# Chapter 6 Y-Chromosome Analysis

## 6.1 MSY biology and paternal-lineage inheritance

The Y chromosome is not wholly non-recombining. Its pseudoautosomal regions (PARs) pair and recombine with corresponding regions of the X chromosome. Most of the intervening chromosome is the male-specific region (MSY), where the forensic Y-STR loci are found [Cou21]. In typical males, the MSY is effectively haploid because most of it lacks a corresponding X region with which to recombine. “Non-recombining” therefore describes the lack of routine X–Y exchange in the MSY, not an absence of mutation or other structural change [Cou21].

A son inherits his Y chromosome from his father. Because MSY Y-STR loci are linked and transmitted together, their alleles are considered as a Y-STR haplotype rather than as independently inherited observations [Kay17c, Cou21]. Men may share a haplotype through common paternal ancestry—identical by descent (IBD)—or carry the same observed haplotype without being closely related, identical by state (IBS) [Kni22]. The profile therefore traces a paternal line, not a unique person [Kay17c, Cou21].

Mutation can alter a Y-STR allele in a descendant, producing differences between men who nonetheless share paternal ancestry, while other parts of their profile retain evidence of that lineage [Kay17c, Cou21]. The MSY loci are not ordinarily reshuffled independently through X–Y recombination [Kay17c, Cou21].

## 6.2 Marker systems, haplotypes, haplogroups, and RM Y-STRs

Routine forensic multiplexes, including Yfiler, PowerPlex Y23, and Yfiler Plus, differ in marker composition and in the rapidly mutating markers they include [Cou21, Ral21]. Broader marker sets can distinguish more observed haplotypes, but a match remains a lineage-level correspondence rather than an individual identifier [Kni22].

A Y-STR haplotype records the STR variants observed across a selected marker panel. A Y-SNP haplogroup is a branch in the paternal Y-chromosome phylogeny defined by shared derived mutations. SNPs can place a paternal line within a branching history and inform ancestry associated with that line; they do not describe a person’s genome-wide ancestry or identify one individual. Haplogroup assignment can also provide investigative intelligence about paternal-line biogeographic ancestry and population history, but its interpretation depends on reference-population representation [Cou21]. STR haplotypes provide variation for comparing sampled male lines, whereas SNP haplogroups provide a more stable phylogenetic framework. Neither result alone establishes a unique person or a specific recent relationship [Cou21, Kni22].

Rapidly mutating Y-STRs (RM Y-STRs) were developed to distinguish paternal relatives whose profiles may be indistinguishable at conventional markers, and can also reduce haplotype sharing among unrelated men. Their higher mutation rates bring an interpretive trade-off: mutation may differentiate close paternal relatives, including a father and son, so a mismatch cannot automatically exclude shared paternal ancestry. Conversely, relatives may retain matching profiles; the number of differences must be considered in light of the markers and genetic distance involved [Bal14, Ral22]. For kinship testing, low-to-medium-mutation markers are recommended, while RM markers are generally considered unsuitable; if RM markers are used, thresholds should account for their mutation rates [Kay17c, Bal14].

RMplex is an expanded research system combining high-mutation-rate Y-STRs, whereas commercial kits include only subsets of these markers. A pedigree study of 1,793 men in 403 pedigrees found that an expanded panel distinguished more patrilineal relatives than a standard panel [Ral22]. An earlier study involving 32 men in five pedigrees reported preliminary differentiation rates and warned that they may be inflated [Ral21]. Even a full match does not specify one person or relationship.

## 6.3 Male-specific profiling in sexual-assault and female-excess samples

Y-STR analysis can recover information from a male DNA contribution that is masked by a much larger female component in autosomal profiling. This is particularly relevant to sexual-assault evidence: semen may be absent even when male cells are present after contact or penetration without ejaculation. A retrospective casework study found that adding Y-STR testing yielded informative male profiles beyond those available from autosomal analysis alone and detected multiple male contributors more often than autosomal profiling [Pur15]. Y-STRs therefore complement, rather than replace, autosomal testing.

Results may address whether a male profile was detected, whether a reference male is incompatible with the observed profile, or whether he cannot be excluded. A compatible profile is a non-exclusion, not identification of one man, given possible paternal-line sharing [Cou21]. Detection does not establish when, how, or through what activity DNA was deposited. Studies have reported background male DNA on women’s underwear, with recovered levels varying with contact context [Cos25e]. In degraded or low-template samples, allelic dropout can yield incomplete profiles and complicate comparison [Cos25e]. Sample choice should reflect the case question and the value of the material, because separate testing can consume limited evidence [Cou21].

Y-STR results may reveal more than one male lineage when autosomal results do not show a clear male component, but they do not guarantee that every contributor can be separated or that contributor number is unambiguous [Pur15, Cou21]. General mixture interpretation is covered in Chapter 3. RM Y-STRs may distinguish some paternal relatives whose conventional profiles are alike, but the RMplex study provided preliminary validation and pedigree evidence rather than establishing routine performance in sexual-assault casework [Ral21]. Its controlled female-excess mixtures support technical promise under those conditions; the study did not test male–male mixtures. The previously typed forensic samples examined also contained enough male DNA to yield informative autosomal profiles, so they did not demonstrate routine RMplex performance in the difficult female-excess case [Ral21].

## 6.4 Haplotype reference databases and frequency estimation

The Y-Chromosome Haplotype Reference Database (YHRD) is a public resource for submitted Y-STR and Y-SNP population data. It supports searches by marker set and population grouping, including national samples and ancestry-based metapopulations; reports should identify the database release and reference set [Wil15b, Roe20]. A count describes recurrence in that sample, not a fixed property of a haplotype. It depends on sample composition and size, population choice and representation, and marker coverage. More loci define more specific haplotypes, but compatible reference data may be fewer. Population choice should reflect the relevant reference question, not solely a suspect’s stated ancestry [Wil15b, Roe20].

The counting method reports the number of exact matches relative to the reference profiles examined. An augmented version adds the target haplotype to both count and denominator, avoiding a zero estimate when no exact match is observed. These transparent approaches link the estimate to the data, but rare counts remain sensitive to sampling variation, database size, and representativeness [Roe20]. No observation in a sample does not mean the haplotype is absent from the population; very few observations likewise do not secure its underlying frequency [Roe20, And13].

The discrete Laplace method uses more than exact recurrence. It models how observed haplotypes lie around inferred central haplotypes, using repeat-step differences to estimate frequencies for observed and unobserved profiles [And13]. This can use similarity among haplotypes when an exact match is absent, but depends on assumptions about mutation and latent ancestry clusters [And13, Roe20]. The model assumes integer repeat counts, excluding null alleles, intermediate alleles, and duplications; the empirical analysis also excluded incomplete haplotypes [And13].

Other approaches include kappa estimates, which adjust rare-haplotype counts using features such as the share of singleton types in a database [Roe20]. Graphical-model estimators represent dependencies among loci: Chow–Liu trees capture relationships between loci, while t-cherry models extend those dependency structures. More complex models face sparse data, and model-based estimates can assign probability to combinations not observed in the reference sample; performance also depends on modeling and population assumptions [And18b, And19].

General likelihood-ratio theory and population-statistical foundations are covered in Chapter 4. No estimator removes the need to assess marker compatibility, population relevance, sample composition, and model assumptions before reporting a frequency [Roe20].

## 6.5 Lineage sharing, match interpretation, and the rare-haplotype problem

Because Y-STRs are inherited together along paternal lines, a matching profile may be shared by paternal relatives. Such sharing may be identical by descent (IBD) from a common paternal ancestor, whereas identical by state (IBS) can produce the same observed profile in men who are not closely related [Kni22]. A match therefore supports correspondence to a paternal lineage rather than distinguishing the person of interest from every possible male source [Roe20, And17b]. The Y-specific challenge is to define an alternative source that represents plausible paternal relationships to the person of interest [And21b].

Mutation affects how many men may share a profile and how they are related. With lower-mutation panels, matching men can include more distant paternal relatives; higher profile mutation rates tend to reduce the number of matches while concentrating them among closer relatives [And21b]. Mutation can also distinguish relatives, so a mismatch does not necessarily rule out shared paternal ancestry. The case’s plausible alternative sources—not simply an abstract population of unrelated men—therefore matter to interpretation [Roe20, And21b].

Frequency-based approaches estimate haplotype recurrence from reference data, using counting or model-based methods. Such estimates describe the sampled data under a chosen population and method; they do not automatically give the probability of a match under the case’s alternative-source proposition [Roe20, And21b]. A haplotype observed rarely or not at all in a database may be poorly characterized when relevant lineages are underrepresented. Database size alone cannot fix unrepresentative sampling or establish how likely a plausible alternative source is to be related to the person of interest [Bre10, And17b, And21b].

Current guidance recommends decision-based evaluation, with quantitative assessment where suitable population data and methods support it, and a qualified qualitative conclusion where they do not [Roe20]. Models have also been proposed to estimate the number and relatedness of males expected to share a profile rather than treating a broad-population frequency as a case-specific match probability. These estimates depend on mutation and demographic assumptions and are not a universal solution [And17b, And21b]. Proposition framing remains debated: Bright and colleagues favor comparing the person of interest with a random man from the population, with the possibility of relatives addressed in the model or statement, while other analyses stress that the population must reflect plausible case alternatives [Bri24, And21b]. The value of a rare match thus depends on how well the selected population or model captures the paternal relatives who could plausibly have contributed the DNA [Roe20, And21b].

## 6.6 Paternal kinship and familial or male-relative searching

Y-STR comparisons can assist when a paternal-line relative, rather than the person of interest, is available for testing. In a paternity-deficiency case, a boy’s profile may be compared with a male relative of an unavailable putative father; in missing-person or historical investigations, remains or an unknown male can be compared with living men from a presumed paternal line [Kay17c, Cou21]. Compatibility supports a paternal-line connection but does not by itself identify the remains or prove which man in that line was the father or other specific relative [Kay17c].

RM Y-STRs can separate some paternal relatives whose profiles match at conventional markers [Ral22]. Because these markers mutate rapidly, they can also produce differences among related men; low-to-medium-mutation Y-STRs are recommended for kinship testing, and RM markers are generally unsuitable for that purpose [Kay17c]. If RM markers are used, mutation-corrected thresholds are needed [Bal14]. Marker choice and the relationship model therefore matter in formal kinship testing [Kay17c, Ral22]. Family-history information may be incomplete or uncertain, which can make a proposed relationship harder to assess and call for independent follow-up.

When a direct autosomal database search has not yielded a source, a Y-STR database search can compare a crime-scene profile with stored male profiles. Unlike familial searching, which generally targets first-degree relatives, Y-STR searching can identify more distant male-line relatives; unlike investigative genetic genealogy (IGG), it uses forensic STR profiles and government databases rather than dense SNP profiles in genealogy databases [Ge20]. Exact or close matches nominate men whose paternal lines may be relevant; close matches require attention to the tested loci and possible mutations, rather than a universal mismatch cutoff [Ge20]. Investigators can examine case information and family records, seek appropriate samples from relevant relatives, and compare a candidate’s autosomal profile with the evidence. A Y-STR hit is a lead for follow-up, not identification of the donor; a direct autosomal comparison or other independent evidence is needed before drawing a source conclusion [Ge20, Kay17c].

As general safeguards, governance should define authorized purposes, retention periods, and disclosure rules. Ge20 describes approved forensic-laboratory access, security and privacy policies, and gaps in legislation specifically governing Y-STR databases [Ge20]. Validated profiles and proportionate handling of relatives’ information can help reduce risks to uninvolved people [Cou21, Kni22]. Search results should not alone justify treating a database match as a suspect or as proof of kinship; candidate relationships and source attribution require independent investigation and testing [Ge20, Cou21, Kni22].

## 6.7 Limitations, decision pathways, and reporting

A Y-STR report should begin from the case question: whether the result compares a trace with a named person, assesses a proposed paternal relationship, or addresses another question. These are not interchangeable evaluations. ISFG DNA Commission recommendations set out a decision-based approach, while earlier guidance treated interpretation only marginally [Roe20]. Later working-group recommendations provide practical reporting detail, but jurisdiction-specific choices should not be presented as universal ISFG policy [Reb20].

For a source comparison, the qualitative outcome is inconclusive, exclusion, or non-exclusion. A non-exclusion may warrant quantitative evaluation only when the propositions, relevant population data, and a suitable frequency method support it; otherwise, a qualified verbal assessment is preferable [Roe20, Cou21]. The report should identify the propositions actually compared, such as whether the DNA came from the person of interest or another man from a specified reference population. Any likelihood ratio (LR) must be explained as the relative probability of the observed evidence under those propositions [Roe20, Reb20]. It must not be presented as the probability that the person of interest is the source; that confusion is the prosecutor’s fallacy.

For transparency, state the markers tested and the observed haplotype, noting whether it is complete or partial and what comparison was possible. If a frequency estimate is given, identify the reference database and population, its release or version and date, and the estimation method [Roe20, Reb20, Wil15b]. A reported frequency describes the result of that chosen data and method; it does not by itself settle the case-specific source question. A matching haplotype is not individualization [Roe20, And21b]. A rare or unobserved database haplotype cannot, on its own, resolve whether a plausible paternal relative contributed the DNA [And21b].

Population representation and profile quality bound the conclusion. If reference data are unsuitable or too limited for a defensible estimate, the report should say so rather than imply precision [Roe20, And21b]. For partial profiles, specify which loci support the comparison and do not treat unobserved markers as matches [Reb20]. For mixed results, state whether a contributor haplotype could be resolved; where it cannot, avoid assigning a single-source frequency to an uncertain profile and limit conclusions to what the mixture permits under applicable guidance [Cou21, Reb20]. A source-level correspondence does not establish when or how DNA was deposited, so reporting should keep the supported comparison distinct from any activity-level conclusion [Roe20, Cou21].

## Closing summary

Y-STR analysis is most useful as a way to detect and compare male-lineage DNA, especially when a large female component limits autosomal interpretation. Marker choice, population representation, sample quality, and the case question shape what a result can support.

A defensible interpretation begins with the case question and makes the comparison, reference data, method, and limitations explicit. Haplotype rarity does not establish individual identity or resolve plausible paternal alternatives, and a database hit is an investigative lead rather than a source conclusion. Reports should state only what the tested profile and evaluation support.
