# Chapter 9 Forensic Epigenetics

## 9.1 The biological basis of forensic DNA methylation

Forensic DNA methylation can provide investigative intelligence—information that may guide follow-up but does not itself establish a person’s identity or an activity. DNA methylation is the addition of a methyl group to DNA, commonly measured at a CpG site, where cytosine is followed by guanine; it changes DNA’s chemical state, not its underlying sequence [Nau23, Vid18]. Methylation can influence gene activity, but effects depend on genomic location and cellular context. Promoter methylation is often associated with reduced transcription, though not invariably, and association alone does not establish gene function [Nau23, Vid18].

Development and cell differentiation remodel methylation patterns. Some persist through cell division, but patterns differ across tissues and cell types [Nau23, Vid18]. Bulk measurements average across cells, so cell composition can shift the measured methylation fraction even when methylation within each type is unchanged [Nau23, Vid18].

Age-related methylation is not uniform: selected CpGs may gain or lose methylation, depending on the sites examined [Hor13, Han12]. This differs from epigenetic drift, the increasing between-person dispersion at many sites with age. A large cross-sectional blood study observed drift but could not show within-person change over time or explain its cause [Han12]. Associations with gene expression or chromatin do not establish causation [Hor13, Han12].

An epigenetic clock combines methylation measurements to estimate age; forensic clocks generally target chronological age. Biological-aging measures instead seek variation in aging processes or related outcomes. A methylation-derived age or age-acceleration score is model-based, not a direct reading of health or overall biological state [Nau23, Hor13, Han12]. A site associated with health or other individual differences may reflect biological variation yet predict chronological age less consistently [Nau23].

## 9.2 Targeted methylation assays and forensic trace constraints

Targeted assays convert methylation differences into sequence or cleavage signals. In the common bisulfite workflow, unmethylated cytosines convert to uracils, read as thymines after amplification, while methylated cytosines remain read as cytosines. The methylated fraction at a CpG is estimated from relative C and T signals or reads [Nau23, Vid18]. Incomplete conversion can make unmethylated cytosines appear methylated; non-CpG cytosines and known-methylation controls can help assess conversion performance [Nau17, Woz21].

Bisulfite enables single-base measurement but can fragment DNA and reduce recovery. Reduced sequence complexity can also cause uneven amplification, particularly when primers overlap variable CpGs or PCR conditions favor one sequence [Nau23, Vid18]. Low-quantity or degraded traces leave fewer starting molecules after conversion, increasing stochastic sampling and amplification imbalance. A minimum input reported for one assay is specific to its conversion kit, workflow, target design, and validation—not a universal threshold [Nau23, Woz21].

Pyrosequencing measures nucleotide incorporation through a light-producing reaction and can quantify individual CpGs across short targets, but is commonly singleplex, limiting throughput [Vid18, Fre20]. SNaPshot, or minisequencing, uses single-base extension and capillary electrophoresis; it can multiplex selected sites, but peak-height ratios are semi-quantitative and can be affected by dye response, primer design, and instrument settings [Vid18, Fre20]. Targeted massively parallel sequencing (MPS) sequences many amplified loci in parallel and estimates methylation from C- and T-read counts. Read depth supports quantitative calls only for molecules that survived conversion and amplification; locus imbalance, library preparation, and platform conditions can affect coverage and estimates [Nau17, Woz21].

Methylation-sensitive restriction-enzyme assays avoid bisulfite treatment by detecting cleavage at selected recognition sites through amplification or fragment patterns. They avoid conversion-related DNA damage, but apply only where suitable enzyme sites occur and report cleavage-dependent outcomes rather than direct base-by-base methylation proportions [Vid18].

Measurements are not automatically interchangeable across platforms. In a shared-sample comparison, EpiTYPER, pyrosequencing, and MiSeq were broadly comparable at the tested CpGs, while SNaPshot showed larger, locus-dependent differences [Fre20]. A collaborative SNaPshot exercise found systematic differences between genetic-analyzer types; PCR buffer and primer conditions also affected measurements. Excessive converted-DNA volume could coincide with reaction failure, plausibly through inhibitor carry-over; conversion-kit effects were not clearly distinguished [Lee21]. Interpretation must match assay and instrument; conversion loss, uneven amplification, and low read depth can compound in low-template or degraded traces [Nau23, Woz21, Lee21].

## 9.3 Chronological-age estimation across tissues

Forensic age models developed from genome-wide methylation screens to targeted panels: array studies mapped age-associated CpGs, then selected loci for focused assays [Nau23, Ref23]. Recurrent markers include **ELOVL2, FHL2, KLF14, PDE4C, TRIM59**, and **MIR29B2CHG** (also called C1orf132 in early work). ELOVL2, FHL2, KLF14, and TRIM59 generally gain methylation with age, while MIR29B2CHG tends to lose it; direction and strength can vary by tissue and CpG [Nau23]. Early blood models combined selected loci; Nau17 used genome-wide data and random-forest-based feature screening to select a broader panel [Nau17]. Zbi15b tested loci selected from earlier work using targeted pyrosequencing and linear regression [Zbi15b].

Linear regression remains common, but methylation–age relationships need not be linear: quadratic regression and power transformations have been used, including for ELOVL2 [Woz21, Bek15]. Random forests can accommodate nonlinear patterns; support-vector machines and neural networks have also been applied. Quantile regression, including quantile-regression neural networks and support-vector machines, can model age-dependent error and prediction intervals [Fre22]. In a blood comparison, quantile-regression neural networks and support-vector models performed similarly; algorithm comparisons remain tied to their markers, cohort, and workflow [Fre22].

Performance depends on data use. Fitting estimates model parameters from development samples; cross-validation withholds parts of that set, while an independent test evaluates a fixed model on samples not used to fit it. A held-out split differs from testing a new cohort or laboratory. The blood-model test and later VISAGE inter-laboratory evaluation illustrate these distinct questions [Nau17, Hei25c].

Errors vary by tissue and study. Held-out VISAGE development results were MAEs—average absolute differences between predicted and observed ages—of 3.2 years for blood, 3.7 for buccal cells, and 3.4 for bone [Woz21]. In a later three-laboratory evaluation, pooled MAEs were about 3.95 years for blood and 4.4 for buccal swabs; blood MAEs ranged from about 2.85 to 5.01 years across laboratories, and the largest individual error was about 15 years [Hei25c]. Tool-dependent MAEs for blood, saliva, and buccal cells ranged roughly from 3.4 to 5.8 years [Sut24]. A held-out semen set had a preliminary MAD of about 5.4 years [Lee15b]; tooth evidence is thinner, with one dentin model reporting 4.86 years without independent testing [Bek15]. The gap between a moderate mean error and a much larger individual error cautions against treating these workflow-specific results as tissue-independent rankings.

An MAE is neither an uncertainty interval nor a guarantee that an individual trace is within that many years; individual errors may be much larger, and the average depends on the cohort and validation design [Nau23, Koo20]. Interpretation is also bounded by the ages represented and tested. Outside a model’s validated range, an estimate is extrapolation, not performance established by its reported error [Nau17, Bek15].

## 9.4 Tissue and body-fluid identification by methylation

Tissue typing asks which cellular or fluid-associated material is present. Differentially methylated sites (DMSs) are CpG sites whose methylation differs across specified cell types or tissues; candidate sites require testing against relevant target and non-target samples [Vid18, Sij21]. Genome-wide screens can identify candidate CpGs for targeted follow-up and complementary loci. Early work used locus ratios; later studies targeted blood, semen, saliva, menstrual blood, and vaginal material [Fru11, For16]. Some developmental studies validated separate markers rather than an integrated multiplex classifier [Sil16].

Classification depends on validating comparator tissues, cell mixtures, and decision rules. Cell composition can shift bulk methylation [Vid18, Ger24]. A marker may distinguish its target from tested comparators yet fail against untested tissues; disease or sequence variation can also affect some signals [Vid18, For16]. In mixtures, intermediate values may suggest multiple sources without identifying components or proportions. Thresholds must be assay- and question-specific; one developmental study called results inconclusive when not all CpGs met its decision rule [Sil16].

Conventional body-fluid tests and RNA typing target different features. Presumptive chemical tests detect activities such as heme-associated catalysis or amylase; antibody tests detect selected proteins, and microscopy can reveal sperm cells. Specificity and performance on aged stains depend on the assay and target. A positive supports the presence of its particular target, not necessarily the whole stain or its donor; mixtures can combine targets or obscure signals [Sij21]. RNA typing measures expression patterns, often across transcripts. Marker overlap and changing expression limit specificity, while mixtures can hide low-level signals. Messenger RNA is more degradation-sensitive than DNA; microRNA may persist better but is not uniformly specific [Sij21, Ger24].

Methylation profiles target DNA and may persist when RNA is unrecoverable, though degradation and processing can still limit success [Vid18, Sij21]. Methylation typing and STR profiling can be co-assessed from one trace, linking the tissue signal more closely to the DNA sample than a protein result; RNA and protein signals are not themselves part of the DNA profile [Vid18, Sij21]. Co-analysis cannot assign a tissue signal to a particular STR contributor in a mixed trace or prove the activity that deposited it. If validated thresholds or reference patterns do not support a class, report inconclusive rather than force an assignment [Sij21, Sil16].

## 9.5 Monozygotic-twin differentiation: promise and limits

Conventional forensic STR profiling generally does not distinguish monozygotic (MZ) twins because they arise from the same zygote and ordinarily share the inherited sequence variation measured by routine STR panels [Vid18, Vid17b]. Methylation offers a different but tentative signal: although twins begin with nearly identical DNA sequences, their methylation patterns can diverge over a lifetime, and genome-wide studies have reported CpG differences within pairs [Vid18, Vid17c]. DMSs that differ between twins are candidate markers, not proof that a trace came from one twin.

An early blood study by Li et al. is included among genome-wide twin comparisons in a review; as summarized there, it supports the observation of methylation variation, not a tested forensic identification procedure [Vid18, Li13]. The forensic proof-of-concept work followed a discovery-to-trace path: researchers screened reference whole-blood samples genome-wide, tested selected candidate sites with a targeted method in reference DNA, and examined those markers in bloodstains prepared as trace-type samples from the same blood [Vid17b]. Some differences persisted, but others weakened, disappeared, or reversed between reference and trace measurements. A marker informative in reference material therefore cannot be assumed to remain informative in a trace; technical variation and cell composition were among possible explanations [Vid18, Vid17b].

The evidence remains preliminary. The primary study used a small, restricted set of twin pairs and blood-derived samples; it did not establish performance across populations, tissues, or real casework traces, nor validate blind assignment of an unknown sample to one twin [Vid17b]. Reviews likewise describe unresolved questions about temporal and tissue stability, marker number, and statistical combination [Vid18, Vid17c]. Evidential assessment would require larger, more varied twin sets; population data on similar patterns in other people; and measures of marker dependence, within- and between-pair variation, and stability across time and relevant sample types. Blind external testing and a validated statistical framework comparing explicit alternatives, such as Twin A versus Twin B, would be needed to quantify evidential weight [Vid18, Vid17b]. Until then, methylation differences remain a proof of concept and potential investigative lead, not an individual-identification conclusion [Vid18, Vid17c].

## 9.6 Exposure inference and smoking as a case study

Methylation can reflect environmental and lifestyle exposures. Smoking is among the better-developed examples: population studies repeatedly link smoking with methylation differences at particular CpGs, including signals that vary with smoking history [Vid17c]. But population-level association is not the same as classifying an unknown contributor from a forensic trace. A marker associated with smoking in one cohort does not by itself establish that it distinguishes smokers from non-smokers in another population, tissue, or low-template sample [Vid18, Ger24].

Maas et al. selected a 13-CpG panel from earlier epigenome-wide association data and evaluated it using methylation-array data, not a targeted laboratory assay [Maa19]. In external validation, AUC was about 0.91 for current smokers but only about 0.70 for former smokers, showing category-dependent performance. The study classified smoking categories using pack-year and cessation-time thresholds; it did not estimate exact consumption or quit date. These population-level results are not a validated call on an unknown trace and do not establish performance on degraded forensic material or in other tissues and populations [Maa19]. Later targeted smoking studies remain research evidence requiring replication before routine forensic use [Ger24].

Interpretation is complicated by the biology and history represented in the sample. Some smoking-associated signals may persist after cessation, while others diminish, so a pattern need not map cleanly onto current, former, or never-smoking status [Ger24, Vid17c]. Cell composition can alter measurements, and blood results should not be assumed to transfer to saliva, buccal material, or other trace types without testing [Vid18, Ger24]. Labels also matter: the Maas study relied chiefly on reported smoking histories, which may be misreported; age, disease, population background, passive exposure, and other correlated factors can complicate an apparent smoking signal [Vid17c, Maa19]. External validation tests generalization but cannot remove uncertainty in reference labels or establish the circumstances of an individual’s exposure.

Predictions of alcohol or drug use, diet, and other environmental or health-related traits remain emerging research directions, not established forensic tests [Vid18, Ger24, Vid17c]. Smoking classification is probabilistic evidence about a methylation pattern, not proof of behavior, exposure dose, timing, or source identity. Because such an inference may disclose sensitive information or invite stigma, ethical analyses urge safeguards against privacy harms and function creep [Sha18, Pal26].

## 9.7 Validation, uncertainty, reporting and privacy

Validation must match the intended forensic use, not merely show that an assay produces a methylation signal. Development should assess analytical sensitivity and specificity, repeatability, and reproducibility, then test the full workflow on relevant specimens and conditions. Marker selection and model fitting must be separated from final evaluation to avoid optimistic performance. Cross-validation can help manage limited development data but does not replace an independent test, external cohort, or inter-laboratory exercise [Nau23, Ref23]. VISAGE age models included held-out samples from their development cohorts; later inter-laboratory testing assessed transfer under different laboratory conditions, a distinct question [Woz21, Hei25c]. As discussed in 9.2, laboratory and instrument effects can shift measurements and estimates [Lee21].

Performance may depend on the sample. Disease and treatment, age, sex, and population background can alter methylation or model error, with effects that vary by application and require testing [Ref23, Koo20]. Cell composition can shift bulk measurements; degradation, low DNA quantity, post-mortem change, storage, and platform differences may further limit transfer [Nau23, Lee21, Ger24]. Validation should specify the specimens, assays, and conditions supported, as well as settings that remain untested or unreliable.

A report should state the specimen and relevant condition, assay and platform, model used, and validated tissue and age range or other applicable domain. For an age estimate, provide a point estimate and a calibrated prediction interval when one has been validated for that use [Fre22], together with relevant validation performance and limitations. The MAE defined above is not an individual error bound: it does not say how far a particular prediction may be from the person’s chronological age [Nau23, Koo20]. For tissue calls, report only classifications supported by validated decision thresholds and sensitivity and specificity studies; where criteria are not met or the sample falls outside the validated scope, report an inconclusive result rather than force a category [Lee21, Ger24]. Results should be framed as investigative intelligence, not identification or proof of activity.

Methylation data can reveal or suggest health- and lifestyle-adjacent information beyond the stated investigative question, including disease-related signals or smoking history [Sha18, Pal26]. Such inferences are probabilistic and may be confounded; they can stigmatize a person or redirect suspicion toward groups. Calling the material crime-scene DNA does not remove the risk: its source may be unknown or innocent, and an age assay may enable later uses beyond its original purpose. Safeguards should be proportionate to the sensitivity and evidential value of the inference. One ethical analysis recommends restricting privacy-infringing analyses and revisiting regulation [Sha18]. A governance proposal recommends purpose-specific legal authorization and independent external oversight for sensitive inferences; these are proposed safeguards, not universal requirements [Pal26].

## Closing summary

Forensic methylation analysis measures biological patterns that may support estimates of chronological age, tissue source, twin differentiation, or exposure history. Each result depends on the sampled cells, assay, model, reference data, and validation conditions. Study-level performance figures do not guarantee the accuracy of an individual result, and a tissue or exposure classification does not establish who deposited a trace or what activity produced it.

These methods are best treated as investigative intelligence with quantified uncertainty, bounded by the intended use and supported validation. Reporting should make the specimen, method, model scope, and uncertainty clear, include an inconclusive option where evidence does not support a category, and protect sensitive health- or lifestyle-adjacent information from disproportionate access or reuse.
