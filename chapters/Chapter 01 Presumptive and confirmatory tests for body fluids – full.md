# Chapter 1 Presumptive and Confirmatory Tests for Body Fluids

## 1.1 Locating Stains and the Logic of Presumptive and Confirmatory Tests

Finding a suspected stain and identifying the material that produced it are separate tasks [Sij21]. Visual examination and alternative light sources (ALS) can help locate stains or narrow the area for later examination. Their signal depends on the stain, illumination wavelength, viewing filter, substrate, and background fluorescence. Semen, saliva, and urine may fluoresce under some conditions, while substrate colour and other fluorescent materials can mask or imitate a signal; a visible or fluorescent area is therefore a lead for testing, not an independent identification. A negative ALS observation does not exclude a stain [Sij21, Rod19, Gam20].

A presumptive test indicates a possible target and helps direct further examination. A confirmatory test supports a more specific claim about a defined component or marker. These labels depend on the question asked: a reaction showing heme-like catalytic activity answers a different question from an assay detecting human haemoglobin. Neither label establishes that an entire stain consists of one fluid, identifies its donor, or explains how it was deposited [Sij21, Cut24]. A positive result supports only the target and sample conditions within the method's validated scope.

Sensitivity is the ability to return a positive result when the target is present; specificity is the ability to return a negative result when it is absent [Mor21b]. Limited sensitivity can produce false negatives, while limited specificity can produce false positives. Performance can also vary with concentration, degradation, substrate, inhibitors, and result-calling rules [Ven14, Mor21b].

A comparison of common blood, semen, and saliva tests found that non-target reactions varied by assay and sample class. Among the examples, luminol and HemaTrace produced reactions in some non-target samples, whereas RSID-Blood and RSID-Semen produced none in the study panel; the saliva tests also differed, with Phadebas Press and RSID-Saliva reacting to several non-target classes [Str23b]. The authors suggest that some reactions of blood-directed tests in faecal and vaginal samples may reflect trace blood within those samples, rather than a response to the matrix itself. They caution against treating RSID-Saliva as confirmatory, and note that the study's sample numbers and procedures limit generalization [Str23b].

Validation error rates describe performance in the validation set. They do not directly state the chance that an unknown stain contains the target after a positive or negative result; that interpretation also depends on the evidence base and case context [Mor21b]. How results from multiple tests are combined and reported is considered in Section 1.7.

## 1.2 Blood: Catalytic Tests, Luminol, and Lateral-Flow Tests

Many presumptive blood tests exploit the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of heme in haemoglobin. In the presence of an oxidant, heme promotes oxidation of a colour-producing reagent. Kastle-Meyer (KM), leucomalachite green (LMG), and tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) are examples. These reactions are not unique to blood: plant materials, metal salts, cleaning products, and other substances can produce or alter them [Tob07, Ven14]. Comparisons do not support a context-free ranking, because observed detection varied with dilution, reagent, reaction-time threshold, sample preparation, and the substances included in each specificity panel. Tobe and colleagues, for example, tested blood from one donor on filter paper; their findings do not predict performance for every stain or substrate [Tob07]. Vennemann and colleagues also reported interference from ferrous sulfate and reduced performance with ascorbic acid under their tested conditions [Ven14].

Luminol is a chemiluminescent presumptive test used to reveal latent or diluted stains and map areas that may not be visible in ordinary light. Heme promotes luminol oxidation and light emission, although the detailed reaction mechanism is not fully settled [Bar07]. Its value is visualization, not confirmation: rust, plant peroxidases, bleach, and some soils, paints, or cleaning materials can also produce light. The faint signal is often documented in darkness, and application can affect stain patterns on some surfaces [Bar07].

Lateral-flow immunochromatographic tests use antibodies to detect a defined antigen in an extract. Most forensic human-blood kits reviewed by Cuttaia and colleagues target human haemoglobin; RSID-Blood targets glycophorin A, a protein associated with human red-cell membranes [Cut24]. Some haemoglobin assays also reacted with primate blood, so the ability to distinguish species varies by assay [Cut24].

A negative lateral-flow result does not necessarily exclude blood. Very high antigen concentrations can produce a hook effect, while ageing, heat, chemical treatment, washing, detergents, pH, or prior presumptive reagents can alter or obscure antibody binding [Cut24].

## 1.3 Semen: Acid Phosphatase, Protein Markers, and Microscopy

Semen contains seminal plasma and, in many samples, spermatozoa. These are different targets: spermatozoa are cells, while seminal plasma is largely formed from accessory-gland secretions; sperm cells make up only a small part of ejaculate volume [Gam20]. A test for a seminal-plasma marker can therefore be positive when spermatozoa are absent or not recovered, and microscopy can fail to reveal cells even when seminal fluid is present [Gam20, Sak20].

Acid phosphatase (AP) is present at high activity in seminal plasma and is used as a presumptive colour test, but it is not specific to semen. Reactions have been reported in vaginal material, faeces, and urine; post-ejaculatory urine can contain semen-associated components [Gam20, Rod19]. The time allowed before reading a result affects the balance between detecting weak stains and observing less specific late reactions [Gam20]. Inhibition is also possible: detergent reduced AP detection in one controlled study, although the authors cautioned against generalizing that result to all detergents [Ven14].

Protein assays provide targets distinct from enzyme activity. Prostate-specific antigen (PSA, also called p30) is a prostate-derived marker associated with seminal plasma and can be useful when spermatozoa are scarce or absent. It is not wholly exclusive to semen; low levels have been reported in other fluids or secretions [Gam20, Sak20]. Semenogelin (Sg), a protein from the seminal vesicles, is the target of the RSID-Semen lateral-flow test [Gam20]. Rodriguez and colleagues observed a positive Sg result in post-ejaculatory urine [Rod19]. This indicates a semen-associated marker but does not by itself determine how it reached the sample.

Microscopy after cytological staining can identify spermatozoa directly. Christmas tree staining and haematoxylin and eosin are among the methods used to distinguish sperm cells from epithelial cells and background material [Gam20, Sak20]. Identification supports the presence of spermatozoa in the examined material. Failure to observe them is less decisive: azoospermia, oligospermia, vasectomy, condom use, low deposition, degradation, or loss during washing and recovery can leave seminal plasma detectable without visible sperm cells [Gam20]. Conversely, a positive protein assay indicates a marker, not sperm-cell presence [Gam20, Sak20].

ALS can help locate suspected semen stains, but its limitations are discussed in Section 1.1 [Gam20, Rod19].

## 1.4 Saliva: Amylase Tests and Their Interpretation

Most conventional saliva screens detect alpha-amylase activity, an enzyme that breaks down starch. In Phadebas tests, digestion of dye-linked starch releases a blue signal. The Press format can transfer a pattern to paper on an item, while the Tube format examines an extract; because the formats and sample handling differ, their results are not automatically interchangeable [Wor18]. Other starch-based approaches use related principles. In each case, the direct observation is amylolytic activity, not necessarily the presence of saliva [Sak20].

Alpha-amylase is abundant in saliva but is not exclusive to it [Sak20]. In a Phadebas paper validation study, the signal depended on concentration, temperature, substrate, and mixture composition. Raising the examination temperature did not improve detection in the tested saliva dilution series, but increased some non-target reactions; blood, urine, and vaginal secretions could also inhibit or weaken detection in mixed stains [Wor18].

Lateral-flow tests for salivary alpha-amylase measure antibody reactivity rather than the enzyme activity detected by starch-based screens. The distinction matters when interpreting results from different methods; comparative findings are summarized in Section 1.1 [Sak20].

## 1.5 Vaginal Fluid, Urine, Sweat, and Faeces

Vaginal-fluid identification remains challenging. The reviewed sources describe no single conventional test with broad validation for routine use; existing approaches rely in part on cellular examination and staining [Sak20]. Lugol's iodine highlights glycogen-rich epithelial cells, but oral epithelial cells can also stain, limiting its specificity for vaginal origin [Sak20]. A questioned sample may contain secretions, cells, or a mixture, so identifying cells associated with a tissue is not automatically equivalent to identifying the entire fluid [Sij21]. Molecular and microbial approaches are discussed in Section 1.6.

Chemical tests and protein markers have been explored for urine and sweat. An ELISA study examined statherin for saliva, dermcidin for sweat, and Tamm-Horsfall protein (THP, also called uromodulin) for urine. The markers distinguished target fluids in liquid samples, but detection was less consistent in prepared stains, particularly for THP. The authors proposed that protein aggregation and extraction efficiency could contribute to weaker recovery from urine stains [Sta11]. Thus, performance in a liquid specimen cannot be assumed to transfer to an aged or substrate-bound trace. The study supports these assays as supplementary approaches, not established replacements for routine screening [Sta11]. Proteomic proposals for urine and other matrices are considered in Section 1.6 [Ste12].

The reviewed sources describe no dedicated routine presumptive test for faeces [Sij21]. Cross-reactions involving faecal samples in tests for other fluids are summarized in Section 1.1. Proteomic work has proposed immunoglobulins as possible faecal indicators, but the markers were not consistently detected and were not validated to the same extent as markers for more commonly examined fluids [Ste12].

## 1.6 Emerging Molecular and Analytical Methods

Emerging methods extend fluid identification beyond enzyme activity, antibody binding, and cell morphology. They examine RNA expression, DNA methylation, microbial communities, proteins, or spectral patterns, with differing levels of development and validation [Sij21].

Messenger RNA (mRNA) profiling identifies patterns of gene expression associated with cell types and fluids. In a EUROFORGEN/EDNAP collaborative exercise, laboratories generally recovered expected marker patterns, but read counts varied across laboratories and platforms; some saliva samples were difficult to distinguish from vaginal secretion because of overlapping markers [Ing18]. A later FoRNAP exercise found that high-input samples were usually easier to classify than low-input or mixed stains, where marker dropout and unexpected signals complicated interpretation. Laboratories also differed in marker panels and interpretation rules [Sal20].

MicroRNA (miRNA) panels offer another molecular strategy, but the literature describes ongoing development rather than a settled, universally implemented method [Sij21, Sak20]. DNA methylation markers exploit differences in methylation patterns among cell types and fluids. Forat and colleagues tested a targeted panel across several fluid classes and mixtures, while documenting biological and sequence-related factors that could alter some signals [For16]. Broader forensic epigenetics is treated in Chapter 9 [For16].

Microbiome methods use microbial DNA patterns associated with body sites or fluids. A pipeline study trained classifiers across sample classes and tested single-source controls, laboratory mixtures, underwear, and other mock samples. Its results show potential for recognizing some components in complex material, but classification varied across classes and mixtures; the authors identify protocol standardization, casework-relevant validation, and uncertainty-aware reporting as unresolved needs [Swa25].

Proteomic methods identify proteins or peptides, often by mass spectrometry. A proposed decision-tree approach reported markers for blood, semen, saliva, vaginal fluid, and other matrices; its blind and case-sample work was strongest for common fluids, while urine and faeces markers were less consistently detected [Ste12].

Raman and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy have been investigated to classify stains from their molecular spectra, sometimes with computational analysis. Their performance can depend on substrate, stain condition, and mixtures, and their use as stand-alone tests requires further validation [Sij21].

## 1.7 Integrating Results, Effects on DNA Analysis, and Reporting

Results from several tests are observations with different targets, not a majority vote: disagreement may reflect different analytes, degradation, uneven distribution, or shared interferents. Their errors should not be assumed independent, and discrepancies should be weighed against sample condition and each assay's limitations [Sij21].

Validation error rates are conditional on known target presence or absence; they do not by themselves give the probability that an unknown stain contains the target after a result [Mor21b]. Bayesian networks can represent several observations and their dependencies. Samie and colleagues tested this approach on proficiency-test traces, but its output depends on the data and assumptions used to build the model [Sam22]. For mRNA mixtures, Ypma and colleagues developed a calibrated approach that allows multiple fluids to be present; its performance depends on training and calibration data and on assumptions used to construct digital mixtures [Ypm21].

Presumptive and confirmatory procedures can affect later DNA analysis, but effects differ by reagent and workflow. Tobe and colleagues reported variation in DNA amplification after six presumptive blood tests; their assessment used defined stains and a limited STR assay, so it does not establish the effect on every exhibit or modern workflow [Tob07]. Luminol is generally reported as compatible with PCR-based STR typing, although treatment has reduced recovered DNA or affected protein tests in some cases; small or compromised stains may be more vulnerable to such losses [Bar07]. These findings support coordinating test order and material use with the intended DNA analysis rather than assuming every screening step is neutral [Tob07, Bar07].

Lateral-flow testing may consume or alter part of a stain, weakening the link between a fluid result and a DNA profile if different portions are tested. Where procedures permit, a shared eluate can make both results refer more closely to the same sampled material. A universal-buffer protocol demonstrated multiple lateral-flow tests and DNA typing from one trace without observed loss of profile quality under its experimental conditions [Bas22]. By contrast, Rodriguez and colleagues found low DNA recovery with the buffer used for RSID-Semen in their workflow, showing that compatibility is method- and sample-dependent [Rod19]. Testing the same material does not prove that the fluid marker and all DNA in a mixed sample came from the same contributor [Sij21].

A clear report should state what material was tested, which target each method addressed, what was observed, and which limitations affect interpretation. It should distinguish a screening reaction from evidence for a defined marker, identify relevant cross-reactions or treatment effects, and explain unresolved discrepancies. Where results are combined through a model, the report should make its assumptions and data limitations clear [Sam22, Ypm21]. Conclusions should not identify a donor or establish circumstances of deposition on the basis of a fluid-marker result alone [Sij21].

## Summary

Body-fluid examinations range from locating stains to testing for defined biological targets, but methods differ in what they detect and in their responses to mixtures, degradation, and interference [Sij21]. Interpretation should state the observed target and relevant limitations, distinguishing fluid evidence from conclusions about donor identity or deposition circumstances [Sam22, Sij21].
