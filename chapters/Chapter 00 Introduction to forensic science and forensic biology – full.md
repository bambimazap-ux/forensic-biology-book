# Chapter 0 Introduction to Forensic Science and Forensic Biology

## 0.1 What Forensic Science Is and What Criminalistics Examines

Forensic science is often described as the application of established disciplines—such as chemistry, biology, physics, or computing—to questions raised by the criminal justice system. Roux, Crispino, and Ribaux argue that this service-based description is too narrow: forensic science also has a distinct object of inquiry, namely traces of past activity and the information they carry about events [Rou12]. The Sydney Declaration similarly frames it as a case-based, research-oriented, science-based endeavour that detects, recognizes, recovers, examines, and interprets traces to understand events of public concern [Rou22]. A trace may be a material remnant, an object, a mark, or a digital signal; it is not defined by its size or by the instrument used to detect it [Rou22].

This perspective does not make forensic science independent of the sciences on which it draws. Rather, it emphasizes that forensic questions arise from particular events: what may have happened, which traces remain, and how well competing explanations account for them. Laboratory results are one part of a wider process that can also inform investigation, connect cases, and assist legal decision-making [Rou12, Rou22]. Criminalistics is closely related, although the terms are not used identically in all traditions. Kirk described criminalistics as a distinct field concerned with evidence and individualization, while historical work on Hans Gross places criminalistics within the developing practice of investigating specific crimes and their material traces [Kir63, Bur13]. Criminology, by contrast, generally studies crime as a social phenomenon, including its causes and patterns; the fields can inform one another but ask different primary questions [Bur13, Rou12].

In Prof. Joseph Almog’s course, the practical work of forensic science was grouped into three roles: establishing whether an offence occurred, supporting or refuting a suspicion, and helping solve a case by connecting findings with investigative leads. The course also distinguished “absolute identification,” such as determining the chemical identity of a drug or poison, from comparative identification, such as comparing a questioned fingerprint, fibre, glass fragment, or DNA profile with a reference. These labels describe different kinds of questions; “absolute” should not be mistaken for a guarantee that every forensic conclusion is infallible. The broader point is that forensic findings can establish a substance, test a suspected connection, or help explain an event, rather than merely name a source.

## 0.2 Historical Development and Founding Principles

Hans Gross helped shape the late-nineteenth-century practice of systematic crime investigation. His *Handbook for Investigating Magistrates* appeared in 1893 and treated the investigation of a particular crime as a disciplined practice requiring preparation, observation, recording, and care in handling traces [Bur13]. Gross should not be presented as the sole inventor of forensic science: the history is gradual, and his work also crossed the boundaries between criminalistics and criminology [Bur13]. Its enduring contribution was to make the scene and its traces an organized object of inquiry, not simply a setting in which investigators gathered obvious items [Bur13]. His recommended discipline included recording the arrangement of objects before moving them, protecting the scene from later disturbance, and treating spatial relationships as potentially informative. These practices also recognize that investigators’ expectations and handling can shape what becomes observable [Bur13].

Alphonse Bertillon developed anthropometric measurements as a means of identifying persons and led the Paris identification service [Can25]. The Dreyfus affair illustrates the danger of carrying authority from one forensic domain into another. When Bertillon became involved in the examination of the handwritten bordereau, he lacked training in handwriting analysis yet proposed that Dreyfus had disguised his own writing. The claim became part of the prosecution’s case, despite disagreement among specialists; Dreyfus was ultimately cleared [Can25]. The episode is a historical reminder that institutional standing, technical ingenuity, or confidence cannot substitute for relevant expertise and defensible reasoning.

The development of fingerprint identification involved contributors including William Herschel, Henry Faulds, Francis Galton, and Edward Henry [Kir63]. The history is therefore better understood as cumulative work than as a single discovery attributed to one person. Edmond Locard’s exchange principle extended the focus from classification toward the traces generated by contact and activity [Rou12, Rou22]. Roux, Crispino, and Ribaux report that Locard’s laboratory was in Lyon [Rou12]. The principle associated with him holds that activity may leave traces behind or carry them away between people, objects, and environments [Rou12, Rou22]. The familiar shorthand “every contact leaves a trace” is not a case-specific conclusion: transfer, persistence, detection, and recovery depend on the circumstances and the trace itself [Rou22].

Paul Kirk’s 1963 account cast criminalistics as the science of individualization and treated the uniqueness of objects as a foundation for source identification [Kir63]. That formulation is historically important, but it does not by itself show that a method can distinguish all possible sources or justify a categorical attribution; those questions are addressed in Section 0.3 [Sak08, Col09].

Beyond its definition of the field, the Sydney Declaration emphasizes the time asymmetry between a past event and its present investigation, uncertainty across the continuum from trace generation to reporting, and contextual interpretation with ethical impartiality [Rou22].

## 0.3 From Individualization to Probabilistic Evaluation

A comparison finding and a source conclusion are different claims. Correspondence describes observed similarity between a questioned item and a reference under the criteria of a particular examination. Individualization is a stronger inference that the questioned item came from one source to the exclusion of all others. Saks and Koehler call the unsupported transition from a reported match to such an exclusive source conclusion the individualization fallacy [Sak08]. Cole likewise argues that the abstract uniqueness of objects does not establish that an examiner can reliably distinguish them in practice [Col09]. A pattern may be rare or distinctive without proving that no other source could produce a comparable observation [Sak08, Col09].

Probabilistic evaluation makes the inferential question explicit. A likelihood ratio (LR) compares how probable the observed evidence would be under two specified, competing propositions: the prosecution proposition, \(H_p\), and the defence proposition, \(H_d\). If \(E\) denotes the evidence, then:

\[
\mathrm{LR}=\frac{\Pr(E\mid H_p)}{\Pr(E\mid H_d)}
\]

The LR expresses the relative support that the evidence gives to one proposition over the other; it does not determine which proposition is true [Eve15, Gil18]. It is not the probability that the suspect is the source, nor the probability that the suspect is guilty. Confusing the probability of evidence given a proposition with the probability of a proposition given the evidence is the prosecutor’s fallacy, also called the transposed-conditional error [Eve15].

The hierarchy of propositions identifies the level of question being evaluated. At the sub-source level, the issue is whose DNA is represented in the profile; at the source level, it is who contributed the biological material [Gil18]. At the activity level, the question concerns what a person did or how material came to be present, while offence-level propositions concern whether the person committed the alleged crime [Coo98, Gil20]. These levels require different information and different alternatives. A DNA comparison may strongly address a contributor question without resolving how or when the DNA was deposited, much less whether an offence occurred [Gil18, Gil20]. Experts should evaluate the highest level that the case information and their expertise support, and state when the available evidence only permits a lower-level assessment [Coo98]. Propositions should represent the competing accounts of the case rather than incorporate the observed result; where practicable, they should be formulated before the comparison is known [Coo98, Gil18]. The information and assumptions on which an evaluation depends should be made explicit, because a change in the circumstances may require the assessment to be revisited [Gil18, Gil20].

## 0.4 Scientific Validity, Admissibility, and the Expert Witness

Scientific validity and legal admissibility are related but distinct. Validity concerns whether empirical evidence supports a method’s claimed performance for its intended use; admissibility concerns whether a court will receive the resulting expert evidence [Scu23]. As summarized by Scurich, Faigman, and Albright, the 2009 National Research Council report raised concern that, apart from nuclear DNA analysis, forensic comparison methods had not been rigorously demonstrated to connect evidence consistently and with high certainty to a particular source. Their account of the 2016 President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology report likewise emphasizes the need for appropriately designed empirical studies and independent replication before claims of foundational validity are accepted [Scu23]. These are summaries of the reports as discussed in that paper, not a substitute for the reports themselves.

In the United States, Frye asks whether a method has gained general acceptance in the relevant scientific community. Daubert instead assigns judges an independent gatekeeping role and identifies flexible considerations that include testability, peer review and publication, error rates and standards, and general acceptance [Edm14]. The Daubert factors are not a mechanical checklist [Edm14]. Edmond and colleagues compared the reception of expert evidence in the United States, England and Wales, Canada, and Australia. They found that formal admissibility rules differed, while leading decisions across the jurisdictions often admitted incriminating forensic evidence and left disputes about reliability to the weight assigned at trial. Their analysis concerns selected leading decisions, not a complete empirical survey of every case [Edm14].

The expert’s task is to explain the observations and the limits of the conclusion [Gil20]. An expert evaluates the evidential value of findings; the expert does not decide guilt or provide the court’s assessment of the probability that a proposition is true [Eve15, Gil20]. The distinction between sub-source and activity-level questions is addressed in Section 0.3.

Prof. Almog’s course used the Demjanjuk litigation to discuss the admissibility of a new method for comparing facial photographs through anthropometric measurements. The course also described the first Israeli DNA-evidence case, in 1993, as an example in which DNA evidence cleared one suspect and implicated another. Both examples illustrate that forensic evidence can be challenged as a method and can test as well as support an investigative suspicion.

## 0.5 Cognitive Bias, Quality, and Accreditation

Forensic decisions are made by people who may be influenced by expectations, prior conclusions, or information about a case. A systematic review found evidence that contextual information and comparison procedures can affect decisions in several forensic disciplines, while also noting that the number and quality of studies vary substantially across fields [Coo19]. The evidence supports taking cognitive bias seriously, but it does not justify assuming that every analyst or every decision is affected in the same way [Coo19].

Linear Sequential Unmasking (LSU) is one proposed way to manage information. It recommends that an examiner document an initial assessment of trace material before seeing a suspect reference, then receive additional information as needed and record changes in interpretation [Dro15]. LSU-E extends the idea beyond direct comparisons by sequencing information according to its potential to bias, its objectivity, and its relevance to the decision [Dro21]. These approaches seek to make information flow more deliberate and transparent; they do not eliminate bias or justify withholding information essential to a sound examination [Dro15, Dro21].

Quality management operates across more than one layer. ISO/IEC 17025 provides a framework for laboratory competence and management, but its laboratory-centred scope does not encompass the entire forensic process, from variable traces at a scene to interpretations about past events [Neu23]. Neuteboom and colleagues argue that accreditation under this framework does not by itself ensure that quality management covers the full forensic continuum, including interpretive work [Neu23]. Method validation must address the method’s intended use and the conditions under which it will be applied; competence, review, and transparent reporting remain necessary parts of quality practice [Neu23].

## 0.6 From Serology to DNA Profiles

Before DNA typing, blood groups and other protein polymorphisms provided biological markers that could help distinguish or exclude possible contributors. Their limits became clear in cases where the question required finer discrimination, including close family relationships [Job13]. This earlier work established a practical role for biological evidence while leaving a gap that DNA analysis would later address.

Alec Jeffreys’s DNA fingerprinting approach used variation in repetitive DNA to produce patterns that could be compared between individuals [Job13, Roe13]. The early method relied on restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis of variable number tandem repeats (VNTRs): DNA fragments were separated by length to create a multi-band pattern [Roe13]. An early parentage and immigration case showed the value of the method where conventional protein markers could not resolve alternative family relationships [Job13]. Its first criminal application linked semen evidence from separate offences, excluded a suspect who had confessed, and eventually helped identify the offender [Job13, Roe13]. This episode established DNA profiling as both an identification tool and a means of correcting an investigative error.

RFLP/VNTR profiling was highly informative but depended on relatively intact DNA in sufficient quantity, and its processing and comparison were demanding [Roe13]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of short tandem repeats (STRs) made it possible to analyze shorter DNA segments and combine multiple loci in a standardized profile [But15, Had21]. The transition was gradual: early PCR assays were more sensitive and faster than RFLP, but initially offered less discrimination; combining multiple STR loci helped close that gap [But15]. The combination of sensitivity, multiplexing, and shared marker systems made STR profiling practical for routine comparison and database use [But15, Roe13]. The core method has continued to develop, but more sensitive detection also makes careful interpretation of mixtures and low-level DNA important [But15, Had21].

Massively parallel sequencing (MPS) adds sequence information rather than relying only on fragment length. It can distinguish STR alleles of the same length that differ in their internal sequence and can combine several marker types within a targeted assay [Bru18, Had21]. This creates new analytical possibilities, but also requires validated workflows, appropriate bioinformatics, and consistent nomenclature before sequence results can be compared and interpreted reliably [Bru18, But22]. MPS expands the kinds of information available; it does not remove the need to assess what a result means in the case at hand [But22].

Prof. Almog’s course used the 2001 murder of Minister Rehavam Ze’evi to show how different forensic disciplines can contribute distinct information. DNA from a cigarette butt at the scene was associated with another butt in an ashtray in a nearby room, but without a database that comparison did not identify a person; a fingerprint developed on a newspaper provided an investigative lead. The course also discussed the 1982 murder of Orly Dubin, in which chromite particles on a suspect’s coat were examined and linked to a foundry, as an example of how trace transfer and possible secondary transfer complicate interpretation. These course examples underline that a biological or material association is not, by itself, a complete account of the activity that produced it.

## 0.7 The Workflow and the Map of What Follows

Forensic biology connects scene work to an expert report through a sequence of documented decisions. Items are located, recorded, collected, packaged, and tracked; the laboratory then examines the material and may test for a body fluid before extracting and quantifying DNA. Suitable genetic markers are amplified and typed, after which analysts assess profile quality, artifacts, contributor structure, and the question the case requires them to address [But22, Had21]. The findings are then evaluated against appropriate propositions and reported with the assumptions and limitations that matter to their interpretation [Gil18, Gil20]. The workflow is not identical for every item: the material, its condition, the amount of DNA, and the investigative question affect which steps are useful and what conclusions can be supported [But22, Had21].

## Summary

Forensic science examines traces of past activity to evaluate propositions relevant to investigation and legal decision-making. Forensic biology applies this approach to biological traces, with methods developing from serological markers through DNA profiles to sequence-based analysis. These advances have expanded the information available for comparison, while interpretation remains tied to the evidence and the case question [Rou12, Rou22, But15, But22].
