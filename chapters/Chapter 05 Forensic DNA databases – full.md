# Chapter 5 Forensic DNA Databases

## 5.1 Purpose, architecture, and marker panels

A national forensic DNA database extends profile comparison beyond a single case. It compares known-person profiles, which are reference records linked to identified people, with forensic-trace profiles, which are unknown-source profiles developed from crime-scene material; it may also link profiles from separate scenes. Some systems support identification of missing people and unidentified human remains through comparison with relatives’ reference profiles. These uses can guide further inquiry; see Sections 5.3 and 5.5 for interpretation of database hits [Ge14b, But12d, But22, But26]. Many systems organize person and trace records in separate indices suited to these different comparisons; the record categories and rules governing their inclusion are addressed in Section 5.2 [Ge14b, But12d].

National systems use different architectures. In the United States, CODIS is the software and network connecting local and state systems with the National DNA Index System (NDIS), the national tier. The UK National DNA Database (NDNAD) is a national collection. European arrangements link separately maintained national databases for cross-border searches rather than replacing them with a single shared database; Prüm is a prominent networked model, discussed in Section 5.7 [But12d, Ama19c].

Database comparison depends on defined short tandem repeat (STR) marker panels. The CODIS core loci and the European Standard Set (ESS) provide shared reference panels; the expanded U.S. core set and expanded ESS share a larger common set of markers [But15g]. Shared panel names do not by themselves ensure compatibility. Direct comparison uses loci present in both profiles, so older or differently configured profiles may offer only partial overlap; allele designations and data formats also affect reliable exchange [But12d, But15g]. STR biology and the rationale for core sets are introduced in Section 2.4.

## 5.2 Record categories, eligibility, and retention

Known-person records may represent convicted people or, in some systems, arrestees or suspects. Some databases also hold missing-person, relative-reference, or unidentified-remains profiles for identification purposes [But12d]. Personnel elimination databases serve a separate quality-assurance role: they compare evidence with profiles of staff and others at risk of accidental contamination, rather than serving as criminal-investigation indices [Nog25]. Comparative work reports variation in whether these systems include only police or forensic personnel, or also visitors with access to forensic areas and non-police personnel in critical roles; it does not establish common retention periods or detailed query procedures [Nog25]. Search and review procedures are covered in Section 5.3.

Inclusion determines whether a profile may enter a database; retention governs how long it remains, and deletion or removal ends its searchable status. These rules vary with offence seriousness, conviction or arrest status, age, and case outcome. European comparisons describe restrictive models that limit inclusion to specified or serious offences and expansive models with lower entry thresholds or longer retention [San13b]. These are broad legislative typologies, not proof of uniform practice. The distinction is not simply convicted versus non-convicted status: restrictive models may include suspects when the alleged offence meets a defined seriousness threshold, while expansive models more often extend eligibility across wider offence categories. Retention is a separate axis. Rules may provide for removal after acquittal or when a record is no longer needed, while convicted-person profiles may be retained for longer periods or indefinitely; age-based review and time limits add further distinctions [San13b].

Retention should fit the database’s stated purpose, and purpose limitation means constraining use to those stated purposes; safeguards against broader use are part of governance [Str19b, Ube24]. Comparative European data did not show that more expansive inclusion reliably yielded more matches per included person [San13b]. The comparison’s limited output measure and uneven cross-country data constrain performance inferences; see Section 5.6 [San13b]. More generally, differences in study design and incomplete reporting of database content limit generalization [Str19b].

## 5.3 Matching workflows, partial matches, and mixture searches

Once a profile is eligible for searching, database software compares it with eligible records under defined matching rules. A full match is agreement across the loci available for comparison. The software returns a candidate hit for review by the laboratories responsible for the records; analysts examine the underlying profiles, resolve discrepancies, and confirm the association before information is passed onward. A hit is an investigative lead, not an identification [But12d].

A partial profile contains results at only some loci; missing loci are not mismatches, but the reduced information can leave more candidates to assess. Moderate-stringency searches use less restrictive agreement rules than routine full-match searches and may return profiles that are not identical. An incidental partial match noticed in an ordinary search is distinct from a deliberate familial search, which is designed to rank possible relatives after authorization; neither result establishes contribution to the crime-scene trace [Piq19, Wei04].

Historically, routine searching was largely limited to single-source profiles or mixtures with a contributor that could be unambiguously separated, because complex mixtures could return many candidates [Bri14e, But12d]. Likelihood-ratio tools extend database searching to mixtures: SmartRank evaluates database profiles as potential contributors, while ProbRank and probabilistic-genotyping searches use quantitative or genotype-based evaluations to rank candidates [Ben17c, Hoo23, Noz24]. This differs from ordering candidates only by matching-allele count, which leaves profiles with the same count tied: SmartRank calculates an LR for each database profile and orders those that pass a chosen LR threshold. In the paper’s CODIS comparison, the search instead used a fixed zero- or one-mismatch rule, illustrating a retrieval criterion rather than an LR ranking [Ben17c]. The cutoff determines who enters the list; the LR ranking determines their order, rather than establishing contribution [Ben17c]. Validation must address retrieval and adventitious associations across relevant profile complexity and database conditions. Lower cutoffs may retain more true contributors but enlarge the list and review workload, whereas stricter cutoffs can omit contributors; difficult mixtures and dropout can also leave true contributors unranked or outside the returned list. No single cutoff fits every case. A Swiss casework evaluation of probabilistic-genotyping searches returned additional associations beyond earlier local comparisons against investigator-selected candidates, but the new associations were not independently confirmed as contributors [Noz24]. Thus a ranked candidate list is a prioritized worklist for profile review and case-specific follow-up, not a verified contributor list [Ben17c, Noz24]. A ranked profile requires expert review and laboratory follow-up before it becomes a usable lead [Ben17c, Noz24]. Interpretation of the mixture itself is covered in Chapter 3, and search-wide evaluation in Section 5.4 [Wei04].

## 5.4 Search statistics, database-wide comparisons, and adventitious matches

The database search problem is the evaluation of a match selected by searching records, rather than a comparison initiated with a named suspect. A named-person comparison asks how the evidence bears on propositions about that person; a database search asks whether a trace matches anyone among the searched records and, if so, whom. The selection process and information available beforehand therefore matter, not only the rarity of a profile [Mee19]. Balding and Donnelly likewise emphasize that the search process and other evidence shape the relevant inference [Bal95].

Search scope and protocol affect what the result means: a search may be open-ended or directed, may include different record categories, and may report a matching person or only the existence of a match. More comparisons create more opportunities for adventitious associations—matches or partial matches between people who are not the source or related to one another. Pairwise comparisons within a database create a different search problem from searching one trace against records. Unrelated profiles can share many alleles, and highly similar pairs become more likely as comparisons grow [Wei04]. Weir’s all-pairs analysis therefore does not give the probability that a particular query profile will match a particular record: a prediction that a highly similar pair exists somewhere in a database addresses a different comparison set. Relatedness likelihood ratios also require prior probabilities and do not, by themselves, establish that two people are related [Wei04]. This possibility alone does not determine the evidential weight of a particular hit [Mee19].

Different formulations of database-match evidence reflect different propositions and prior information. A database-wide search does not call for a universal correction applied mechanically according to database size, and a targeted search does not necessarily make every database member equally plausible. Under a database-centred formulation, the familiar \(1/(np)\) expression depends on an open search in which no person was singled out and database members are treated as equally plausible trace donors; a suspect-centred \(1/p\) formulation can be more natural when a candidate was already targeted or others have been excluded [Mee19]. Whether the report identifies the matching person and supplies that person’s match probability, or reports only that a unique match exists, also affects which probabilities can be evaluated. Evaluation should follow the actual search protocol and the question being asked [Mee19]. A search result can point to a candidate for further investigation; evidential evaluation requires a comparison under stated propositions [But24b]. Chapter 4 develops the probability and likelihood-ratio foundations rather than re-deriving them here [Mee19].

## 5.5 Familial searching: methods, limits, and ethics

Familial searching is a deliberate search of a forensic DNA database for a possible relative of the source of an unknown-source profile, usually after a direct search has found no match. It generates an investigative lead, not an identification, and differs from an incidental partial match in a routine search [Piq19, Ge20].

Candidate profiles can be ranked by allele-sharing methods or by a kinship index, a likelihood ratio comparing the genetic data under a specified relationship with the data under an unrelated-person hypothesis. Close relationships such as parent–child or full siblings are common targets; the statistical foundations are covered in Chapter 4, Section 4.5 [Kim11c, Ge20]. Thresholds determine how many candidates proceed: permissive settings may retain more true relatives while enlarging the candidate pool, whereas stringent settings may reduce false leads while missing relatives. Follow-up can include Y-chromosome STR or mitochondrial DNA testing for lineage concordance, investigative checks, and direct comparison of an appropriate reference profile with the trace; marker methods are introduced in Chapter 6 [Kim11c, Ge20].

Performance depends on profile quality, database composition, the assumed relationship, and population frequencies. Rohlfs and colleagues show that population structure and a mismatched reference population can make relatives harder to distinguish from unrelated people [Roh12]. Simulations of a particular protocol found that more distant Y-haplotype-sharing relatives could be returned as apparent close-relative leads, potentially redirecting scrutiny to their immediate families [Roh13]. Candidate rank or a kinship statistic therefore does not prove a precise relationship. Because some groups are overrepresented in databases, their relatives may face disproportionate scrutiny [Roh13, Kim11c].

Safeguards described in U.S. and England-and-Wales practice include limiting searches to eligible serious unsolved cases, exhausting routine searches and other leads, requiring authorization, and limiting disclosure [Piq19, Kim11c]. See the opening paragraph of this section for the interpretation of such leads [Piq19, Ge20]. Searches can extend inquiry to relatives who are not in the database, raising family-privacy concerns [Ge20, Mat20c]. Proponents emphasize potential investigative value in serious unresolved cases; critics stress privacy, unequal exposure, and false or overstated leads. Statistical controls do not settle this policy balance [Kim11c, Mat20c, Piq19].

## 5.6 Performance measures and investigative value

Performance measures must match a database’s stated purpose. Profile and trace counts describe coverage, not success. A hit rate usually relates person–trace matches to loaded crime-scene profiles, while other measures use person records or the population as their denominator; each answers a different question. Santos and colleagues found no reliable association between broader inclusion and person–trace matches per included person, but that measure did not assess resolution, deterrence, or cost [San13b]. Inclusion and retention shape database content, but size alone does not establish value.

A warm hit may confirm a person already under investigation; a cold hit may provide a new lead. UK reviews note that match statistics do not consistently distinguish these categories, and that a hit may be confirmatory or unrelated to the offence [Ama19, Ama21]. A hit is not a solved case; evaluation should follow its progression through investigative action and later outcomes, recognizing that each stage has a different denominator and an associated outcome does not establish causation.

Clearance measures reflect many investigative and institutional factors. The systematic review reports positive findings across studies of detection, clearance, and deterrence, but varied designs, crime types, outcome definitions, and incomplete descriptions of database content limit comparison and attribution [Str19b]. It also reports mixed results, including lower homicide clearance in one observational comparison and negative conviction-rate effects despite increased clearance and charge rates in another study [Str19b]. UK assessments find that DNA can advance individual cases, while its aggregate contribution to recorded-crime outcomes and independent causal effect remain difficult to establish [Ama19, Ama21]. Case histories illustrate cold-case use but do not, without consistent search and resolution counts, estimate success rates [Mes18].

Deterrence measures, such as reoffending or population crime rates, address a different question from hit and clearance measures; proxy outcomes and possible displacement complicate their interpretation [Str19b]. Cost and resource assessments must also account for collection, analysis, database operation, and follow-up. Wickenheiser’s benefit figures are projections from observed hits under assumed prevention and monetary values, not measured outcomes [Wic22]. The policy question is whether added investigative value justifies resources and breadth of inclusion, alongside proportionality and retention [Str19b, San13b].

## 5.7 International exchange, governance, privacy, and legitimacy

Transnational exchange includes shared international stores, linked or networked national databases, request-based exchange, and hybrid models. These differ in where profiles are held and how searches or disclosures occur, while national rules remain central [Ama19c]. The categories describe different channels rather than mutually exclusive national choices: shared repositories include the INTERPOL DNA Gateway, which holds profiles without associated personal information, and the Europol Information System, where DNA profiles may sit alongside crime-related and subject information subject to access restrictions. In a linked model such as Prüm, profiles remain in national databases; request-based arrangements use bilateral or other national channels, and many countries combine these approaches [Ama19c]. Request-based exchanges may likewise use a profile-search stage followed by further information after a match, but the permitted search and disclosure depend on the agreement and national law [Ama19c]. Prüm illustrates a networked, two-stage process: an automated search returns a hit/no-hit result, after which the searching authority may request identifying or further case information [Ama19c, Too19]. In the first stage, crime-scene stain and person-reference DNA profiles are compared without names or case files being part of the automated exchange; after a hit, personal data may be requested from the state holding the matching file, with further information obtained through national procedures that vary by country [Too19]. See Section 5.3 for confirmation and interpretation of database hits [Too19]. Interoperability enables compatible systems to exchange or compare profiles; it does not harmonize national inclusion or retention rules or ensure uniform follow-up [Too19, Mac20e].

Governance safeguards include purpose limitation, controlled access, data security, retention and deletion rules, and independent oversight. International governance proposals emphasize privacy-risk review, transparency, and assessment of new techniques, while leaving implementation to national systems [Ube24]. Accountability also requires reporting that distinguishes initial from confirmed hits and tracks follow-up, including false or unproductive leads; hit counts alone cannot show investigative or prosecutorial value [Too19, Ama19c]. These are governance needs, not evidence that current systems meet them.

Public legitimacy concerns whether database use is seen as acceptable and trustworthy, but cannot be inferred from popularity or match counts. A scoping review found recurring support for crime-control uses alongside concerns about privacy and improper access, while noting a small and heterogeneous evidence base, often drawn from non-representative samples. Such surveys do not establish informed, general, or enduring consent [Mac19b]. A broader account of forensic genetics in crime governance cautions against conflating technical capacity and security aims with demonstrated effectiveness or democratic accountability [Mac20e]. Elimination databases remain a distinct contamination-control layer; their governance matters before profiles enter national or cross-border searches, although comparative evidence does not show a uniform pre-search workflow. See Chapter 2, Section 2.1 [Nog25].

## Summary

Forensic DNA databases are investigative systems whose value depends on record rules, search and review procedures, appropriate statistical evaluation, and proportionate governance. Direct, mixture, and familial hits require the interpretation described in Sections 5.3 and 5.5 [But12d]. Cross-border interoperability supports comparison without harmonizing national rules [Too19, Mac20e].
