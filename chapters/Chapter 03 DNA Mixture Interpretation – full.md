# Chapter 3 DNA Mixture Interpretation

## 3.1 Recognizing a Mixture and Assessing Suitability

Mixture recognition is a profile-level judgment, not a decision made from one signal in isolation. More than two distinct alleles at a locus can support the presence of multiple contributors, but an additional signal must be assessed against the profile's overall pattern and plausible artefacts. The practical framework of Clayton and colleagues and later recommendations emphasize evaluating loci in the context of the full profile rather than treating a single allele count as conclusive [Cla98, Gil06].

The absence of an excess of alleles at one locus does not rule out a mixture. Contributors may share alleles, causing their signals to overlap; a weak component or partial profile may also conceal evidence that would otherwise distinguish contributors. Conversely, an apparent extra allele may have another explanation. Mixture recognition therefore depends on the combined pattern across loci and on the quality of the observations [Cla98, Hu14].

Recognizing a mixture is separate from deciding whether it is suitable for interpretation or comparison. A profile may contain DNA from multiple people yet provide too little reliable information to distinguish or compare its components. Other profiles may support interpretation of some loci or components but not the whole profile. Suitability is judged under laboratory criteria supported by validation, not by the mere presence of reportable signals [Sci21]. The decision need not be all-or-nothing across a profile: some loci or components may support further assessment while others do not. The relevant question is whether the retained data support the interpretation being made, rather than whether every part of the profile can be assigned [Sci21].

Where practicable, the evidentiary profile should be interpreted before reference profiles are considered. This helps keep the initial assessment of possible genotype combinations independent of knowledge about a person of interest; comparison follows once the profile itself has been assessed [Gil06, Sci21].

## 3.2 Estimating the Number of Contributors

The number of contributors (NoC) is an estimate of how many people are represented in the observed profile, not always a value that can be read directly from it. A common starting point is the maximum allele count (MAC): the greatest number of distinct alleles observed at any one locus. As discussed in Section 3.1, allele sharing and weak components can conceal contributors; accordingly, MAC provides a lower bound [Pao05, Han11]. Artefacts can also make a profile appear more complex [Hu14]. Low-template data and replicate variation can leave plausible contributor counts overlapping [Per11].

Other approaches use information beyond the maximum count at a single locus. Perez and colleagues examined allele counts across loci and replicate amplifications; Haned and colleagues compared MAC with a maximum-likelihood estimator that uses allele-frequency information. Other proposed approaches include decision trees, machine learning, and cumulative allele-count methods [Per11, Han11, Kru20, Ben19, Noe22]. These approaches estimate NoC; they do not, by themselves, measure the evidential weight of a profile. Their outputs depend on the information and assumptions available and should not be treated as certainty about the number of biological contributors.

In simulations, maximum likelihood performed better than MAC for some more complex mixtures, while relative performance depended on mixture size, allele-frequency information, and profile completeness [Han11]. That study used simulated profiles with allele calls already identified; it did not establish performance across all casework conditions. Empirical count-based guidance likewise found overlap between contributor groups, particularly in low-template data [Per11]. Such methods can organize the evidence and make assumptions explicit, but no count estimate should be mistaken for a direct observation of everyone who contributed.

An estimate may be stated as a specific count, a range, or a minimum, depending on what the data support; the form of the report should not imply greater precision than the profile allows [Hic23, Sci21]. Hicklin and colleagues caution that agreement with a known contributor count does not show that the count was discernible from every profile, and an estimate that differs from the known count does not alone establish that a comparison conclusion was wrong [Hic23]. In MIX13 Case 5, a deliberately constructed four-person mixture had no more than four alleles at any locus, so allele count alone would make it appear to contain two contributors; no participating laboratory classified it as a two-person mixture [But18].

## 3.3 Relative Contribution and Major and Minor Components

When one contributor is represented by stronger signal across the profile, that component may be described as major and the weaker one as minor. Relative peak heights or areas, considered with heterozygote balance (the relative peak heights of the two alleles at a heterozygous locus; see Chapter 2) and the pattern across loci, can help assess whether a proposed division of alleles is plausible. These observations support an approximate assessment of mixture proportion, the relative signal attributed to a component, not a direct measurement of the amount of DNA originally contributed by each person [Cla98, Gil06].

Gill and colleagues showed how allele peak areas can be used to compare candidate assignments in simple mixtures. Where four distinct alleles are observed, a proposed pairing can be assessed by comparing the relative areas assigned to each component; at loci with fewer observed alleles, an estimated proportion may help compare alternative pairings. In either case, the estimated proportion depends on which alleles have first been assigned to each component; it cannot independently prove that assignment or establish a unique contributor genotype [Gil98b]. Candidate assignments should be considered across informative loci and against empirically supported expectations, rather than inferred from one peak or a universal numerical rule [Gil06, Lec04, But06].

Signal can vary among loci, and amplification and sample characteristics also affect the observed pattern; an estimate from one locus need not represent the profile as a whole [Cla98, Gil98b]. As discussed in Section 3.1, shared-allele signals may contain contributions from more than one person, so a peak does not by itself identify each person's share [Cla98, Gil98b]. A major component may be easier to recognize than a minor one, which can remain ambiguous [Gil06]. The assignment of complete contributor genotypes is a further question, addressed in Section 3.4.

## 3.4 Deconvolution and the Use of a Known Contributor

Deconvolution is the process of identifying possible contributor genotype combinations that could explain the observed mixture. For a two-person mixture, an examiner can consider candidate allele pairings at each locus and ask whether the assignments fit the signal pattern and remain coherent across the profile. Candidate assignments that conflict with the relative signal or with the pattern at other loci may be less plausible, but the remaining alternatives still need to be carried forward if the data do not distinguish them [Cla98, Gil06]. As discussed in Section 3.1, more than one combination may remain plausible; an assignment should not be presented as unique unless the data support that conclusion.

As noted in Section 3.1, the evidentiary profile should be interpreted before reference profiles are considered [Cla98, Gil06]. A reference that matches one proposed component can support the view that its donor cannot be excluded, but it does not by itself resolve all other possible combinations [Cla98, Gil06]. A distinct procedure is conditioning on a known contributor: the analyst explicitly assumes that a person, such as a victim, contributed and uses that person's genotype to constrain the remaining possibilities. This is an assumption that can narrow the set of explanations, not independent proof that the person contributed [Cla98].

Conditioning does not physically separate the contributors; incomplete data may still leave more than one genotype combination possible [Cla98, Hu14]. Thus, comparison with a reference and conditioning on that person's contribution answer different interpretive questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

As the number of contributors rises, the possible genotype allocations multiply, even when the observed alleles themselves are clear. This makes it harder to decide which alleles belong together and to check whether proposed component profiles remain consistent across loci. Lynch and Cotton argue that manual deconvolution should generally not be attempted for mixtures of more than two people, except where a clear major contributor can be identified. Their combinatorial analysis explains the growth in possible assignments; it is not a claim that every higher-order mixture is impossible to interpret by any method [Lyn18].

## 3.5 Low-Template Mixtures and Interpretation Thresholds

In low-template mixtures, a minor contributor may be represented by few cells; stochastic allele loss and masking by taller major-contributor peaks can make that component difficult to recover [But12c]. Replicate amplifications may differ, and heterozygote imbalance can make peak-area interpretation more difficult as the analyzed DNA amount falls [Gil00, Gil15]. Replicate-based consensus rules favor reproducible calls but may omit genuine alleles that fail to recur. Consensus and pooled profiles can also produce different calls: consensus retains reproducibility-based allele calls, whereas pooled profiles can preserve signal-strength information. The methods therefore offer different information, and combining calls can recover additional alleles while also requiring care about extra signals [Gil00, Ben11b]. Replication supports interpretation without making the contributor set certain; very low-template results may represent contributors visible in the data rather than everyone who contributed biologically [Per11].

Degradation is distinct from low quantity: fragmentation reduces successful amplification of longer STR targets, leaving fewer loci available to distinguish contributors or compare a profile [But12, Sci21]. A missing allele or weak component alone does not establish non-contribution, and comparisons may need to rely on the subset of loci that remains interpretable [But12, Sci21].

Interpretation thresholds and related criteria must be validated for the laboratory's methods and intended mixture conditions, including the contributor numbers, proportions, and template levels the laboratory plans to interpret; they are not universal values or guarantees that every relevant allele has been detected [Sci21, But12c]. In mixtures, signal strength alone may not settle whether an allele is present, because sharing and other profile features affect interpretation [Sci21]. The 2012 ISFG recommendations call for drop-out and drop-in probabilities based on method-representative validation data, but their simplified framework does not fully address complex mixtures; statistical treatment is covered in Chapter 4 [Gil12]. Replicate handling and signal inclusion should likewise follow validated laboratory procedures [Sci21]. If the data are too limited or poor in quality to meet the laboratory's suitability criteria, comparison may not be appropriate. Where interpretable data do not support a defensible inclusion (the reference cannot be excluded under the laboratory's criteria) or exclusion (the reference is excluded under those criteria), the result should remain inconclusive [Sci21].

## 3.6 Interlaboratory Variation and Professional Guidelines

Collaborative interpretation exercises show that laboratories and analysts do not always agree on whether a mixture is suitable for interpretation, how many contributors it represents, or which alleles can be assigned to a component. The NIST MIX05 and MIX13 studies also found variation in comparison outcomes, especially for complex mixtures and minor components; some disagreements occurred among analysts following the same laboratory approach. These exercises document variation under their tested conditions; they are not universal estimates of casework error [But18]. They used selected electronic profiles and voluntary participation rather than a representative census of routine casework, which further limits broad generalization [But18].

A later multi-laboratory study likewise found differences in suitability assessments and NoC estimates [Hic23]. Suitability decisions can determine whether an interpretation is reported at all. The distinction between an estimated count and whether that count is discernible from the profile is discussed in Section 3.2 [Hic23]. Differences may reflect laboratory policies and procedures, interpretation thresholds, allele-retention rules, analytical systems, training, and professional judgment; no single cause explains every disagreement [But18, Hic23].

ISFG and SWGDAM guidance provide shared principles for structured interpretation, documentation, validation, and quality control. Both stress that assumptions and interpretation criteria should be supported by relevant empirical data and laboratory procedures [Gil06, Sci21]. SWGDAM describes binary interpretation as treating alleles or genotypes as present or absent, and cautions that this approach has added limitations for complex mixtures, stochastic-level contributors, and related people [Sci21]. Such guidance can make decisions more transparent, but it does not provide a universal answer for every profile or remove the need for local validation, training, review, and defensible judgment [Gil06, Gil12, Sci21]. The consequences of contributor-number decisions for statistical evaluation are taken up in Chapter 4 [Hic23].

## Summary

Mixture interpretation proceeds through linked but distinct judgments: whether a profile supports multiple contributors, whether it is suitable for comparison, how many contributors the data support, and whether any contributor genotypes can be resolved. Allele counts, relative signal, and reference profiles can narrow the possibilities, but none alone guarantees a unique interpretation. Shared alleles, weak components, incomplete data, and methodological choices preserve uncertainty.

Validated laboratory procedures and professional guidance structure these judgments, while collaborative studies show that meaningful variation remains. The defensible interpretation is therefore the one that reflects the information the profile supports, states unresolved alternatives, and avoids treating an estimate or a plausible genotype assignment as certainty.
