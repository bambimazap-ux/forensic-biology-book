# Chapter 4 Statistics of DNA Profile Analysis

## 4.1 Allele and Genotype Frequencies and Population Databases

An allele-frequency database records allele copies observed in reference samples typed at specified loci. Its estimates describe the sampled population and depend on the sample design, population labels, laboratory methods, and quality of the genotype records; they are not universal constants [Bud01b, For03]. Curated datasets therefore document provenance and sampling, and screen for duplicates, close relatives, incomplete or implausible profiles, and inconsistent allele designations [Bod16]. Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) relates allele frequencies to genotype probabilities within a population model, while linkage equilibrium is the assumption that loci are sufficiently independent for their genotype probabilities to be combined. The product rule uses those assumptions to calculate a multilocus profile frequency from locus-specific genotype frequencies [For03, Bal94, But24b].

A profile-frequency estimate is thus conditional on the reference data and genetic assumptions, rather than a direct count of complete profiles or a statement about a particular person. Population samples with distinct origins should not be treated as interchangeable merely because they were typed at the same loci [Bud01b]. Hardy–Weinberg checks can reveal departures that merit investigation, including possible sampling or technical problems, but a departure alone does not show that a dataset is invalid [Bod16]. Database quality checks and population descriptions make the estimate more traceable, but cannot establish that every relevant subgroup is represented [Bod16, Bud01b]. Population structure and finite-sample uncertainty require additional treatment, discussed in Section 4.2 [Bal94].

## 4.2 Population Structure and Theta

The product rule presumes allele draws from the modeled population are independent. Population substructure can make allele copies more correlated when people share ancestry within subgroups; recent close relatedness is a distinct, more specific dependence and may call for an explicit relationship model [Bal94]. The coancestry parameter θ, also associated with FST, represents population-level correlation under a defined comparison. In the Balding–Nichols model, it adjusts genotype probabilities to allow for this structure; the appropriate value depends on the populations and markers in question, so θ is not a universal constant [Bal94, Bal05b]. It does not replace a model for a known close relative.

NRC II recommendations belong to the historical shift toward explicit population-structure modelling. Their influential “2p” adjustment addressed a particular concern about an apparently homozygous, single-band VNTR result when a second band might have gone undetected; it is not a general θ correction and was not intended as a rule for PCR-based genotypes [Eva97, Bal94].

Finite-database uncertainty is separate: another finite sample from the same target population could yield different frequency estimates, and confidence or credible intervals quantify that sampling uncertainty under their assumptions [Cur02, Cur05]. Such intervals do not themselves model population structure. A minimum-frequency floor may be applied in calculations according to laboratory practice, but it is not a universal rule and does not correct for population structure [Bod16]. Database quality controls support use of the data; they do not remove either source of uncertainty [Bod16].

## 4.3 From Profile Rarity to Likelihood Ratios

A random match probability (RMP) describes the expected frequency of a profile in a specified population under stated genetic assumptions. A likelihood ratio (LR) compares the probability of the same observed DNA results under two competing propositions [For03, But24b, Hic22]. These quantities answer different questions: profile rarity alone is not the evidential weight of a case-specific comparison [Bal95, But24b].

At the sub-source level, propositions concern who contributed the DNA. They should state competing explanations independently of the observed match—for example, that the profile came from the person of interest or from an unknown, unrelated person. Wording a proposition as “the matching DNA came from” the person of interest builds the result into the proposition and can make the comparison uninformative [Gil18].

For a full single-source profile, if the person of interest is assumed to be its source and laboratory error is set aside, the probability of the results under that proposition may be close to one. If the alternative is an unrelated person from the specified population, the probability under that alternative may be represented by the RMP; in this restricted setting, the LR may approximate the inverse of the RMP [For03, Bal95]. This is not a general identity: a close-relative alternative or different conditioning information can change the comparison [Bal95].

A small RMP is not the probability that the person of interest is not the source [For03]. The LR expresses how much more probable the findings are under one stated proposition than the other; it does not establish which proposition is true [For03, Hic22]. The comparison is conditional on the information and assumptions used, including the alternative relationship and relevant population model. Changing those conditions can change the LR without changing the observed profile [But24b].

## 4.4 Likelihood Ratios for Mixtures

For an interpreted mixture, a mixture LR compares the probability of the observed profile under competing sub-source propositions. These may specify a person of interest and an unknown contributor under one proposition, and unknown contributors under the other; a known contributor can be included under both when justified by the case information [Gil06, Gil18]. The propositions should state the disputed contribution, relevant relationships, and contributor-number assumptions. Mixture recognition and deconvolution are covered in Chapter 3, especially Section 3.4.

Under each proposition, compatible genotype combinations can be summed or integrated, with unknown contributors weighted by genotype probabilities from the relevant population-frequency model rather than assigned a single guessed genotype [Wei97, Gil06, Mor20b]. A known contributor may constrain those combinations, but treating that person as a contributor is an explicit conditioning assumption, not a conclusion established by the calculation [Gil18]. The contributor numbers specified under the competing propositions need not be equal; each must be justified in light of the case and findings [Gil06, Wei97]. The LR can be sensitive to that contributor-number assumption [Gil18, But24b].

Random-man-not-excluded (RMNE) and combined probability of inclusion (CPI), with combined probability of exclusion (CPE) as its complement, summarize the proportion of a population not excluded by a set of observed alleles [Gil06, Bie16f, But24b]. They are not comparisons of POI-specific and alternative propositions. Reducing a mixture to binary inclusion can discard distinctions among genotype combinations and does not itself represent uncertain peaks or possible missing alleles; the result remains conditional on the allele and locus decisions made. CPI guidance excludes loci where drop-out remains reasonably possible, illustrating how the inclusion result also depends on interpretation choices [Bie16f].

## 4.5 Kinship and Pedigree Likelihoods

In kinship analysis, the competing propositions specify biological relationships. The paternity index (PI) compares the probability of the genetic findings if the tested man is the father with their probability if an unrelated man is the father [Mor02, Gje07]. If a close relative is a plausible alternative, that relationship should be modelled explicitly: shared ancestry can make the findings more probable under that alternative and reduce the LR. As in Section 4.3, a PI is evidential weight rather than a probability of paternity; posterior odds also require prior odds and specified alternatives [Gje07].

Missing-person and disaster-victim identification can use pedigree likelihoods to assess whether remains occupy a specified position in a family tree, against an explicit alternative such as an unrelated person or a different relationship [Ge10]. Profiles from several relatives can be evaluated jointly because their genotypes are linked by inheritance; untyped relatives may be included as unknowns where needed [Ge10]. Alternative pedigrees can also be compared, but the best-supported pedigree is only the best among those specified, and its ranking can change with omitted alternatives or different prior assumptions [Ege00]. Joint assignment methods in large disaster-victim identifications can help avoid incompatible individual assignments, conditional on the family pedigrees used [Vig21].

These calculations depend on population-frequency assumptions, possible mutation, the accuracy of family links and sample attribution, and the completeness of the available family profiles [Ge10, Gje07, Pri07, But12g]. A mutation model may allow a genuine relative to remain compatible despite an apparent mismatch, but its form and assumptions must be specified [Gje07, Ge10]. Incomplete family references can leave several pedigrees difficult to distinguish, and the candidate pedigree set bounds what can be concluded [Ege00, Pri07]. A pedigree LR supports one stated relationship model over another to the extent indicated by those inputs; it does not prove the relationship [Ege00, Ge10].

## 4.6 Semi-Continuous Models for Drop-Out and Drop-In

Binary inclusion or exclusion summaries can treat alleles as simply present or absent, a limitation when the profile is incomplete or uncertain [Bie16f, Cob19]. Semi-continuous models instead assign probabilities to the observed allele pattern under each proposition, allowing the likelihood calculation to account for drop-out and drop-in without directly modelling quantitative peak heights [Gil12, Gil06]. The underlying interpretation of these events is covered in Chapter 2, Section 2.6, and their casework consequences in Chapter 3, Section 3.5.

For a proposed genotype, the model evaluates how probable the called pattern would be given the relevant event probabilities. Under an alternative involving unknown contributors, likelihoods can be averaged over possible genotypes weighted by population frequencies [Gil12, Inm15, Gil06]. LRmix Studio and Lab Retriever illustrate this general approach, though implementations and parameterizations differ; for example, a shared drop-out input does not represent all biological variation among loci and contributors [Cob19, Inm15].

These models use allele presence, absence, or uncertainty, not the relative magnitudes of peak heights as evidence in the likelihood. Drop-out and drop-in probabilities are method-dependent, not universal constants, and should be supported by validation data relevant to laboratory procedures, thresholds, and sample conditions [Gil12, Inm15, Cob19]. Values drawn from another laboratory or an earlier method may not describe the process under evaluation [Gil12, Inm15]. Sensitivity to plausible drop-out and drop-in values should therefore be examined [Gil12, Inm15, Cob19]. Continuous models are discussed in Section 4.7.

## 4.7 Continuous Probabilistic Genotyping, Validation, and Reporting

Continuous probabilistic-genotyping models use quantitative peak heights as well as allele calls to compare possible contributor-genotype configurations [Cow07, Man20b]. Relative signal can help distinguish configurations that explain the same called alleles. Depending on the model, peak-height variation, stutter, degradation, drop-out, and drop-in may be represented, but not in the same way by every system [Cow13, Gil21c, But24b]. Section 4.6 describes models that do not directly evaluate peak heights.

EuroForMix and DNAStatistX use gamma-distribution peak-height models and maximum-likelihood estimation, while STRmix uses a Bayesian framework and Markov chain Monte Carlo to integrate over uncertain parameters. These are broad computational distinctions, not a guarantee that implementations, components, or outputs are interchangeable [Gil21c]. Cow07 describes an early gamma model of peak areas, while subsequent work extended treatment of artifacts such as stutter and drop-out [Cow07, Cow13].

Validation addresses whether a model is scientifically justified for its intended inference, whether software implements its specification accurately, and whether the laboratory’s procedures support its intended casework use [Cob16, Bri15b]. These are related but distinct questions: a sound model does not ensure correct code, and correct code does not establish performance across the laboratory’s intended casework range. Testing should use relevant known-sample data and representative as well as challenging conditions, with discrepancies investigated and the validated scope made clear [Cob16, Bri15b]. Model fit and contributor-number assumptions matter because they can change an LR; a high LR alone does not establish analytical reliability [Gil21c, But24b]. Calibration and reproducibility testing are informative but do not guarantee performance on a different case or sample set [Bri15b, But24b].

Agreement across repeated analyses or laboratories does not by itself establish that a model is adequate, and comparisons have reported variation, especially for complex profiles [Gil21c, But24b]. Reports should identify the propositions, material assumptions, population data, model and software version, and relevant limits; they should make clear that the LR is not the probability that either proposition is true [Mea20, But24b].

## 4.8 Evaluation Given Activity-Level Propositions

The hierarchy of propositions distinguishes sub-source questions about whose DNA is represented from activity-level questions about what activity deposited it and when [Coo98, Gil20]. A sub-source LR may support that a person contributed DNA, but it does not by itself address how or when the material arrived. As in Section 4.3, an LR compares findings under propositions; it is not the probability that an activity account is true [Gil20].

Evaluation considers the path from activity to observation through transfer, persistence, prevalence, and recovery (TPPR). Transfer concerns movement between people and objects; persistence concerns whether material remains available over time; prevalence addresses the possibility of relevant material already being present in the background; and recovery concerns what the collection and analytical process detects [Tay18, Oor19, Tay21]. DNA may move directly or through secondary, tertiary, or further transfer, and background DNA may also contribute. Material must persist and then be recovered through the case-specific collection and analytical process; consequently, the same finding may be expected under more than one account [Tay18, Oor19, Tay21].

Experimental studies can inform probability assignments, but their relevance depends on how well their participants, substrates, contact histories, sampling, and analytical methods fit the case [Tay18, Oor19]. The ReAct study found substantial variation among laboratories in recovery and resulting evaluations, limiting direct transfer of recovery data and LRs between laboratories [Gil25b]. Experimental findings therefore inform the conditions studied rather than universal transfer or recovery rates.

Bayesian networks and related frameworks make activity pathways, background sources, dependencies, findings, and assumptions explicit; sensitivity analysis can identify which uncertain inputs drive an evaluation [Tay18b, Tay21, Sam20]. Such structures cannot supply reliable probabilities where suitable data are absent. Guidance recommends clear propositions tied to the disputed activity, relevant case information, and transparent assumptions and limitations [Gil20, Buc25]. Published recommendations differ on numerical versus qualitative reporting when data are limited [Buc25].

The defensibility of activity-level evaluation remains contested. Supporters argue that it can prevent a sub-source result from being mistaken for an answer about deposition; concerns include sparse or case-mismatched data, false precision, and uneven training and practice [Bie16c, Ken26, Oor19]. A critique of a particular fingernail-DNA lookup table cautions against weakly supported propositions and numbers, rather than addressing every activity-level method [Sch26b]. Where propositions are unclear, key probabilities lack support, sensitivity depends on weak assumptions, or relevant competence is absent, the limits should be stated rather than masked by a numerical result [Tay18, Gil20, Buc25].

DNA statistics provide conditional comparisons whose meaning depends on propositions, population assumptions, profile uncertainty, and method validation. Activity-level conclusions additionally depend on whether the supporting data fit the case.
