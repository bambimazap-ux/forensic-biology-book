# Chapter 8 Biogeographical Ancestry and Forensic DNA Intelligence

## 8.1 Genetic variation, population structure and ancestry

Biogeographical ancestry (BGA) inference is a form of forensic DNA intelligence: a probabilistic result used as an investigative lead to guide follow-up, but not to establish identity, ethnicity, social identity, or individualize a person [Job22, Tve22]. Its genetic basis is that allele frequencies differ among sampled populations. Because ancestry panels select loci for their ability to distinguish those samples, their signal is not a neutral picture of genome-wide variation and may make group differences appear sharper [Job22]. A reference population is a sampled group used as a comparison in an analysis; conclusions are conditional on which groups are represented.

Human variation can show gradual geographic clines—changes in allele frequencies across space—while local discontinuities may also occur. Serre and Pääbo treat such local breaks as exceptions to broad gradients, not evidence of continental divisions; the apparent Native American–East Asian break in their analysis coincided with a gap in sampling of North American Native American individuals [Ser04]. A cluster is a model-dependent grouping, not a self-evident human category. Its appearance depends on the markers, sampled groups, model settings, and geographic scale [Ser04, Pri00c, Ros05].

Admixture estimates describe genetic contributions relative to the clusters represented in an analysis, not a person’s literal family tree. Similar-looking proportions may reflect different histories, including recent mixture, drift, or an unsampled source; they do not by themselves identify ancestors, dates, or migration events [Pri00c, Law18]. The interpretation therefore remains bounded by the reference data and the model’s fit [Tve22, Law18].

## 8.2 Ancestry-informative markers and forensic panels

An ancestry-informative marker (AIM) is a genetic marker selected for its ability to distinguish specified comparison populations. Its usefulness depends on the populations, question, and intended resolution: a locus that separates broad groups may add little information about neighboring ones, and its ranking can change with the comparison set [Ros03, Kid14].

Marker-selection measures capture different properties. Allele-frequency difference (δ) measures contrast for a specified pair of populations; informativeness for assignment (Iₙ) measures how much an allele’s frequency pattern reduces uncertainty about source population across the selected groups. Neither measure alone establishes how well a panel estimates several ancestry components. FST summarizes population differentiation, rather than the precision of an individual estimate, while effective allele number (Aₑ) describes diversity rather than necessarily between-group discrimination [Ros03, Che19e]. Fisher information concerns precision in estimated ancestry contributions and depends on proportions and population diversity as well as frequency contrasts [Pfa04]. Population-specific divergence (PSD) measures a target group’s divergence from others; cumulative PSD can help assess whether a panel’s signal is balanced across groups [Phi14].

Panel performance depends on combining complementary markers, not merely collecting loci with the largest pairwise contrasts. A panel can over-weight an easy distinction while providing weaker information for other groups. The EUROFORGEN Global AIM-SNP design sought balanced differentiation across broad reference groups while giving greater priority to some regional contrasts than to fine separation among others [Phi14]. Comparisons of SNPs and microhaplotypes likewise show that rankings and performance depend on the metric, marker type, and populations tested [Che19e].

Panel size must suit the intended task and assay. Compact panels may support broad distinctions, while closer populations require markers targeted to those differences; technical performance and reference-population coverage matter alongside marker informativeness [Phi21, Ter24]. More markers cannot compensate for omitted or poorly represented populations. Even a panel evaluated across diverse samples may be intended only as a first-tier tool for broad regions, with finer resolution requiring better-targeted markers and sampling [Kid14].

## 8.3 Statistical assignment, clustering, and uncertainty

An ancestry analysis may compare a profile with named reference populations or explore structure among sampled individuals. Likelihood classification compares observed genotypes with population allele-frequency data; Bayesian classification combines likelihoods with prior probabilities to estimate support for candidate groups. Both are conditional on the listed references: the top-ranked group may fit poorly, and a classifier can select a winner even when the relevant source is absent [Tve22, Mog19].

Principal component analysis (PCA) summarizes major axes of variation and locates profiles relative to sampled references; its coordinates are not probabilities of origin or forensic assignments [Tve22, Kay23]. STRUCTURE and ADMIXTURE are model-based clustering methods that estimate cluster frequencies and individual membership or ancestry proportions. STRUCTURE uses Bayesian estimation, whereas ADMIXTURE maximizes likelihood under a related model [Pri00c, Ale09]. Their outputs depend on the selected number of clusters, sample composition, and model assumptions. Assumptions such as Hardy–Weinberg proportions within clusters and independent, unlinked markers may be violated by continuous geographic variation, linkage, or other departures, affecting the result [Pri00c, Ale09]. A plotted component should therefore not be read as a natural group or literal genealogy [Pri00c, Law18].

A clean plot does not guarantee good model fit. An outlier or rejection test checks whether a profile is compatible with the available references and allows an analyst to withhold an assignment when none fits or several remain plausible. In a Precision ID evaluation, GenoGeographer could report no assignment or ambiguity, unlike naïve maximum-likelihood ranking, which always selected a top population; this reduced discordant calls while producing more inconclusive results [Mog19]. An operational service also grouped predicted roots into broad reporting categories rather than making precise ancestry claims [Jin18]. Missing genotypes need explicit treatment: excluding profiles with inadequate marker coverage and accepting partial profiles are different choices, and the latter requires validation of how missing loci affect estimates [Mog19].

Classification uncertainty—uncertainty about whether a profile can be reliably assigned to a represented group—is especially important for admixed profiles. In South American populations, individual estimates varied across marker sets even when population-level patterns were more stable [Per20]. Reporting should distinguish a visualization or cluster membership from classification against named references, state when several groups remain plausible, and permit a finding of poor fit rather than forcing a label [Tve22, Mog19, Kay23].

## 8.4 Forensic panels and MPS integration

Massively parallel sequencing (MPS) can place distinct forensic marker classes in one assay or workflow. Identity-oriented STRs and SNPs support profile comparisons; AIMs support population comparisons; and markers for externally visible characteristics (EVCs) support predictions about selected traits. Sharing a platform does not make these separate inferences interchangeable or turn ancestry and appearance outputs into identity evidence [Ter24, Kay23].

The EUROFORGEN Global AIM-SNP set and Precision ID Ancestry Panel are ancestry-focused tools, not identity or appearance panels. Their results remain tied to their reference populations and intended geographic scope [Phi21, Ter24, Kay23]. ForenSeq-based approaches illustrate a broader workflow in which identity-oriented markers and ancestry- or phenotype-predictive SNPs are analyzed through one sequencing system, but each marker set feeds a different inference [Ter24, Kay23].

MAPlex combines ancestry-informative SNPs and microhaplotypes in an assay designed to improve differentiation across Asia-Pacific populations, particularly East and South Asian and Near Oceanian groups. Its development study did not establish operational casework validation for challenging traces or mixed samples [Phi19]. VISAGE Basic combines ancestry SNPs and pigmentation predictors in an MPS multiplex, while the Enhanced Tool broadens the ancestry-and-appearance scope with additional marker classes and trait predictors [del21, Rui23]. The five-laboratory evaluation of the VISAGE Enhanced Tool reported 99.8% inter-laboratory genotype concordance under the study conditions; this technical agreement does not validate every downstream ancestry or appearance inference [Xav22b]. Population analyses also identify limits in reference coverage and complex-admixture resolution; mixture applications remain preliminary [Rui23].

## 8.5 Prediction of externally visible characteristics

Forensic DNA phenotyping predicts selected appearance categories from genotype, most firmly established for eye, hair and skin colour. Systems such as IrisPlex, HIrisPlex [Wal13], and HIrisPlex-S return probabilities across defined categories, not a direct measurement or complete description of a person [Cha18]. These categories simplify traits that vary continuously; pigmentation is also affected by factors such as age, sex, exposure, and body site [Ter24].

The area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) summarizes how well a model distinguishes a category from alternatives across decision thresholds. The review reports approximate AUC ranges of 0.74–0.99 for eye colour, 0.64–0.94 for hair colour, and 0.72–0.99 for skin colour across systems and trait categories, not for a single model [Sch19]. An AUC is not the percentage of cases classified correctly and is not the probability that a particular person has the predicted trait. Published performance varies by trait, model, and category; intermediate eye colour, for example, is less predictable than blue or brown, and performance also varies across hair and skin categories [Ter24, Kay23, Sch19]. Positive predictive value (PPV) describes how often a positive category call is correct in a specified validation setting; negative predictive value (NPV) describes the corresponding reliability of a negative call. Both depend on category definitions, thresholds, and the validation data, so they are not universal constants [Sch19]. High AUCs elsewhere in the reviewed systems do not ensure a useful positive prediction for every difficult category: for IrisPlex intermediate eye colour, the review reports an AUC of 0.74, a PPV near 0.09, and an NPV near 0.96 [Sch19].

For a case, the relevant output is the set of category probabilities for that profile, not an average AUC or a generic predictive value. Probabilities should be read together: a leading category may be qualified by a substantial probability for a neighboring category [Cha18]. Intermediate eye-colour profiles may, for instance, receive similar probabilities for blue and brown rather than a high probability for the intermediate category [Sch19]. A leading result is not automatically a confident or exclusive description.

Validation data also bound the inference. Reviews note uneven population representation and the need for broader evaluation, particularly for underrepresented and admixed groups [Ter24]. Genetic prediction concerns selected, categorized traits rather than every influence on appearance; hair colour, for example, can change with age [Ter24, Sch19]. These results may help narrow an investigative field, but they cannot identify anyone or warrant treating a category probability as a description of one individual.

## 8.6 Interpreting ancestry results as investigative intelligence

Operationally, an ancestry result is a conditional comparison against specified references, not a claim about a person’s identity, birthplace, ethnicity, race, culture, or appearance. Clusters and admixture proportions are model outputs, not natural or exhaustive human categories or literal genealogies [Job22, Law18].

Interpretation is harder for admixed profiles and at subcontinental scales. Neighboring or genetically overlapping populations may not be separable, so a broad regional signal may be supportable where a precise country, community, or parental origin is not [Tve22, Kay23]. Uncertainty marks the boundary of what the comparison can sustain; it is not a reason to supply a more definite label.

Operation Minstead illustrates the cost of excessive precision. Early analyses of Delroy Grant’s DNA suggested a specific Caribbean origin, including a lead toward Trinidad, despite limited and opaque marker and reference data and overlap among admixed Caribbean populations. Police resources were directed toward several Caribbean islands. A later analysis found the profile consistent with predominantly African and some European ancestry, while the authors conclude that the ancestry and phenotype analyses did not meaningfully advance the investigation [Phi26]. The case does not show that ancestry analysis is invariably unhelpful; it shows how an uncertain comparison can become a misleadingly precise geographic story.

The EDNAP exercise exposed a related interpretive risk without testing real investigative outcomes. Laboratory reports converged for straightforward profiles but varied for more complex or admixed cases, offering competing regional and admixture descriptions. Some reports also used predicted skin tone to support an ancestry interpretation, although phenotype and ancestry are distinct inferences [Die26b]. Analyst choices and wording can thus turn uncertain outputs into apparently settled descriptions. A qualified comparison—not a person’s label—may be the most supportable result.

## 8.7 Operational use, reporting, governance, and ethics

Operational validation evaluates the complete BGA capability, not merely successful genotyping. Poulsen and colleagues recommend defining the investigative purpose and resolution, then assessing the marker panel, reference populations, genotyping process, and analysis tools together. Validation should address relevant casework conditions, including admixture, missing data, and profiles poorly represented by the references; procedures should specify when results are qualified or withheld. These are operational recommendations, not a universal standard [Pou26].

Different kinds of evidence about readiness must not be conflated. The EDNAP exercise compared how laboratories interpreted and reported shared BGA and EVC outputs; it did not validate assays, measure real-world investigative outcomes, or establish official reporting rules [Die26b]. Atwood and colleagues assessed outsourced providers for a specific New South Wales Police context, including prediction, reporting clarity, limitations, and service suitability. Their recommendation for specialist review is specific to that jurisdiction and service, not a validation of every provider or method [Atw21].

A case report should identify the assay and marker panel, validated scope, analysis method, and reference populations, including important gaps in coverage. It should state whether the output concerns BGA or an EVC, how uncertainty and competing or unsupported assignments were handled, and the intended investigative use. Performance claims should be tied to relevant validation data rather than presented as certainty about an individual [Die26b, Pou26, Atw21].

Scudder and colleagues propose an intelligence-cycle approach: define the question, assess the result alongside other information, communicate uncertainty, and seek independent corroboration before operational action. A DNA-derived lead should be checked through identity-focused comparison, not treated as identity evidence; this framework is a proposal rather than a tested intervention [Scu19c]. Their proposed model privacy impact assessment asks agencies to justify necessity and proportionality, limit access and secondary use, map retention and disclosure, and revisit safeguards as capabilities change; it is not a binding or empirically tested protocol [Scu18].

Governance must account for disagreement about social acceptability. Civil-society interviews found varied views about investigative benefit, privacy, discrimination, stigma, and acceptable limits, but do not establish a representative public view or common threshold for use [Sam19a]. Professional-stakeholder interviews likewise found wide variation in which phenotypic tests were viewed as ethically or socially problematic and in the criteria used to draw that distinction [Sam18]. Wienroth’s analysis describes professional community-building and anticipatory self-governance, not proof of technical reliability or consensus [Wie18]. Legal authority is separate: Zieger describes differing European national approaches and broader rights frameworks, not one Europe-wide operational rule or a global one [Zie22]. Use should therefore rest on an explicit, reviewable purpose, proportionate access, transparent reporting, and safeguards against biased targeting and tunnel vision. BGA and EVC predictions are investigative leads, never evidence of identity and never a basis for individualization [Pou26, Scu19c, Scu18].

## Summary

BGA and appearance-prediction methods extract probabilistic signals from selected genetic markers and compare them with defined reference data. Their outputs depend on marker choice, sampling, model assumptions, population coverage, and validation; broad patterns may be informative even when fine-scale assignments or individual trait calls remain uncertain.

Their proper role is investigative intelligence. Clear reporting, proportionate governance, and independent follow-up can help prevent a model-dependent comparison from becoming an unwarranted claim about a person. Neither ancestry nor appearance inference establishes identity, and neither individualizes.
