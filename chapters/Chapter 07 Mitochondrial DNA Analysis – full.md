# Chapter 7 Mitochondrial DNA Analysis

## 7.1 Mitochondrial genome, maternal inheritance and forensic value

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), also called the mitogenome, is a compact, circular genome outside the nuclear chromosomes. It contains genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation, protein synthesis, and mitochondrial gene expression. Its non-coding control region contains regulatory elements involved in replication and transcription, including the D-loop, and includes sites with much of the variation used in forensic comparisons [Cou21b, Amo19b].

Cells can contain many mtDNA copies because mitochondria hold multiple genome molecules and cell types differ in mitochondrial abundance [Cou21b, Amo19b]. This high copy number can improve the chance of recovering usable templates when nuclear DNA is scarce or degraded, including from rootless hair shafts, teeth, and skeletal remains. It does not guarantee a complete or interpretable sequence, and it improves recovery prospects rather than individual-level specificity [Car26b, Cou21b].

Under the usual pattern of maternal inheritance, mothers transmit mtDNA to their children, and the genome is treated as non-recombining for forensic comparison; variants therefore tend to travel together through maternal lines [Amo19b, Cou21b]. Reports of paternal transmission remain debated and do not displace this usual forensic model [Car26b, Cou21b]. Maternal relatives can provide useful references when a direct reference is unavailable, but a shared sequence cannot distinguish among people who carry the same maternal-line profile. A match therefore supports correspondence to a maternal lineage, not unique identification of one contributor; people without a known maternal relationship may also share a sequence [Amo19b, Car26b].

Sample collection and extraction are covered in Chapter 2. The general lineage-marker framework is introduced in Chapter 6.

## 7.2 Target regions and sequencing strategies

Forensic targets range from the control-region hypervariable segments HVS-I and HVS-II to the complete control region and the whole mitogenome. HVS-I and HVS-II are established, compact targets, but leave out variation elsewhere. Sequencing the full control region adds HVS-III and intervening sites; whole-mitogenome analysis also covers coding regions and may add discriminatory variants and phylogenetic resolution [Cou21b, Tem13, Car26b]. More sequence can improve comparison [Hol21b], but a whole-mitogenome match is not necessarily unique to one person [Cou21b, Tem13, Car26b].

Sanger sequencing reads selected, PCR-amplified regions through chain termination. Forensic use has commonly focused on HVS-I and HVS-II or the broader control region; extending Sanger analysis across the whole mitogenome is less straightforward [Cou21b, Car26b]. Massively parallel sequencing (MPS) reads many DNA fragments in parallel and can target the control region or the whole mitogenome. Tiled-amplicon designs use short, overlapping PCR products to cover successive regions, avoiding reliance on a single long product and supporting analysis of compromised samples [Hol21b]. Validation studies assess defined workflows: the FBI PowerSeq study covered the control region, the ForenSeq study evaluated separate control-region and whole-mitogenome workflows, and Cihlar et al. reported developmental validation of a specific whole-mitogenome workflow [Bra19, Hol21b, Cih20].

For highly fragmented material, hybridization capture enriches target fragments from a prepared DNA library with complementary probes before sequencing, rather than requiring PCR amplification of each region as an intact target. Templeton et al. demonstrated recovery of whole mitogenomes from severely fragmented remains [Tem13]. A missing-person implementation study likewise found gains and limitations relative to its laboratory’s Sanger method [Cue20].

Broader coverage and greater sensitivity can reveal more variation, but do not by themselves establish call accuracy, guarantee reliability, or make a result uniquely identifying. Casework use depends on validation of the specific platform and end-to-end workflow for relevant samples; the FBI study supports its control-region assay, Holt et al. validate the specified kit–instrument–software workflows, and the missing-person study reports gains and limitations relative to its laboratory’s Sanger method [Bra19, Hol21b, Cue20]. Performance should not be assumed to transfer between methods.

## 7.3 Contamination and mtDNA-specific safeguards

The sensitivity that makes mtDNA useful when nuclear DNA is scarce also makes low-level exogenous DNA consequential. A trace contribution or carryover between amplification steps can create misleading signal, particularly when compromised samples contain few endogenous templates. As noted in Section 7.2, greater sensitivity can expose low-level signals that need to be distinguished from contamination or technical noise [Cou21b, Car26b].

Laboratories use separated pre- and post-amplification work areas, dedicated equipment and reagents, and contamination controls [Grz18, Par14c]. Extraction blanks and negative amplification controls can reveal contamination introduced during processing or amplification; positive controls show whether a workflow produced an interpretable result. The ISFG recommendations call for controls and extraction-reagent blanks throughout the process [Par14c]. Staff haplotype records can help identify profiles consistent with personnel contamination [Grz18]. A signal in a blank does not alone determine whether a case result is usable; its relation to the evidentiary profile and case context requires cautious assessment [Grz18].

Nuclear mitochondrial DNA segments (NUMTs) are mitochondrial-like sequences embedded in nuclear DNA. They can be co-amplified or co-enriched and mistaken for mtDNA variants, especially where short fragments or enrichment methods limit their distinction [Mar21d, Car26b]. Linked variants resembling a known NUMT pattern may raise concern, but mixtures, genuine mtDNA variation, and some NUMTs can resemble one another; no single read-level feature is decisive [Mar21d]. Post-mortem damage can also create apparent substitutions. Cytosine deamination produces characteristic changes, and damage-associated patterns in degraded fragments can help distinguish them from reproducible sequence variation [Mar21d, Cou21b]. Sequencing errors and inconsistent alignment can also produce false calls or mismatches.

Sequence calls are strengthened by redundant data and review rather than sensitivity alone. Where appropriate, replicate sequencing—preferably across both strands or with different primers—can test whether calls persist. Independent review of raw data can catch transcription and interpretation errors [Par14c, Grz18]. Electronic transfer from analysis software, traceable records, and limited manual re-entry reduce avoidable data-transfer mistakes [Par14c, Grz18]. Phylogenetic consistency checks can prompt review of unexpected patterns but cannot replace examination of reads and controls [Grz18, Car26b]. Regular mtDNA proficiency testing provides an external check on laboratory performance [Par14c, Grz18].

## 7.4 Point and length heteroplasmy

Point heteroplasmy is the coexistence of different nucleotide variants at one mtDNA position. Length heteroplasmy is a mixture of mtDNA molecules that differ in the number of repeated bases, most often in control-region homopolymeric tracts. Point heteroplasmy appears as alternative bases at a site; length heteroplasmy appears as a distribution of sequence lengths, which can be difficult to represent consistently in repetitive cytosine tracts [Par14c, Stu19].

Heteroplasmy proportions need not be the same across tissues. Developmental segregation can leave variants at different levels in different tissues, and variant proportions can differ among hairs from one person [Par14c, Gaa20]. In a study of buccal references and rootless hairs, variants that were minor in a buccal sample sometimes predominated in individual hairs. MPS revealed some variants that were below Sanger detection in the reference, showing how an apparent hair–reference mismatch can arise without a different maternal lineage. The study’s sample set and assay do not establish a universal pattern for all people or workflows [Gaa20].

Sanger and MPS do not yield interchangeable heteroplasmy calls. Sanger detection depends on sequence quality, position, and peak interpretation; peak proportions need not directly represent variant proportions, so fixed detection thresholds are not appropriate across laboratories [Par14c]. MPS can detect lower-level point variants, but calls depend on validated, platform-specific thresholds and data quality; low-level signal may also reflect technical noise or other artefacts [Gaa20]. Length-heteroplasmy patterns are especially method-sensitive, with differences reported across sequencing technologies and analysis workflows, particularly in homopolymeric tracts [Stu19, Ewe26].

Neither heteroplasmy nor a single sequence discrepancy automatically excludes a person or maternal relative. The ISFG guidelines state that heteroplasmic differences do not by themselves exclude otherwise identical haplotypes, and call for laboratory interpretation and reporting rules suited to the technology and sequence quality [Par14c]. In a multigenerational whole-mitogenome study, treating heteroplasmic variants as differences excluded known maternal relatives, whereas guideline-based treatment avoided false exclusions in that pedigree [Con22b]. This supports cautious, method-aware comparison, not a universal threshold.

## 7.5 Alignment, nomenclature, haplogroups and phylogenetic checks

Mitochondrial sequence differences are conventionally reported against the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS), which supplies stable coordinates. The rCRS is a reporting reference, not an ancestral or “wild-type” human genome [Par14c]; it represents a particular sequence and retains rare polymorphic alleles [And99]. It anchors notation but does not determine the biologically best alignment in every region [Par14c].

Notation describes observed sequence differences relative to the reference. Substitutions are reported by position and observed base; insertions use decimal positions, and deletions are explicitly marked as deletions. Point heteroplasmy is represented with an appropriate IUPAC ambiguity code, while mixed insertion or deletion states follow the relevant guidelines [Par14c]. These symbols describe sequence observations, not their evidential weight.

In repetitive tracts, especially homopolymers, an insertion or deletion may have more than one apparent placement. Minimizing differences from the rCRS alone can make related sequences appear to have different placements, inflating mismatches, distorting database counts, and misleading comparisons [Ban06]. Phylogenetic alignment uses harmonized conventions informed by the mitochondrial phylogeny; where alternatives remain plausible, phylogenetic evidence and weighted parsimony can guide the choice [Par14c, Ban06]. Consistent notation matters for reports and database searches, because different encodings of the same sequence can hinder comparison. SAM2 uses alignment-independent string searches to find equivalent sequences with differently placed indels, while standardized phylogenetic alignment supports interpretable reporting [Hub18, Par07d].

Haplogroups are branches of the mtDNA phylogeny, characterized by combinations of linked variants. Comparing a sequence with the expected motif of a candidate branch provides a plausibility check: an unexpected pattern can prompt review of reads, alignment, or sample records, but does not by itself prove an artefact [Par14c, Dur21]. PhyloTree is a curated framework of haplogroup motifs and relationships that informs alignment and sequence-consistency checks [Par14c, Hub18, Dur21]. Control-region data lack diagnostic coding-region variants and generally support less resolved haplogroup assignments than complete mitogenomes; a broad placement should not be mistaken for a definitive subclade assignment [Par07d, Dur21].

## 7.6 EMPOP, population data and evidential weight

EMPOP (EDNAP mtDNA Population Database) is a forensic reference resource for comparing a case haplotype with curated population data and estimating its occurrence in those data; it is not an identity database [Par07c]. Its value depends on trustworthy and comparable entries. Sequence vetting, data provenance, redundant review, and phylogenetic checks can expose transcription errors, unexpected variants, or possible artificial recombinants. An anomaly prompts review of underlying data rather than automatic exclusion [Par07c, Par14c, Grz18]. SAM2’s alignment-independent search complements the harmonized sequence conventions described in Section 7.5 [Hub18].

A search returns an observed haplotype count within a selected database and sequence range. That count describes the database, not a universal population frequency. If no match is observed, the supported frequency is not zero: estimates must allow for finite sampling, and alternative estimators or upper confidence bounds can produce different evidential weights [Par14c, Buc11]. Database choice matters. Population data should be assessed for sequence quality, sampling design, and geographic or ancestral representation. A large database may still poorly represent plausible contributors in a case, while non-random sampling or population structure can distort an apparently precise frequency [Par07d, Par07c, Buc11, And21b].

A likelihood ratio (LR) compares the probability of findings under competing propositions, as developed in Chapter 4. For mtDNA, a database-derived haplotype frequency may inform the probability of a match under an alternative-source proposition; treating its inverse as the LR requires assumptions about the relevant population and the source’s relationship to the known person [Par14c, Buc11, And21b]. The alternative source may be a maternal relative who shares the haplotype, so a match supports lineage correspondence but generally cannot distinguish among people with that haplotype [Buc11, And21b]. It is not the probability that the named person was the source.

Developing methods address limits of simple frequency summaries. Simulation approaches estimate distributions of the number and relatedness of people who may share a mitogenome, conditional on database counts and explicit mutation or demographic models [And18c, And21b]. A recent collaborative study proposes incorporating observed profile discrepancies between tissues into an LR framework, but presents a preliminary model rather than a settled reporting standard [Cla26]. Such outputs depend on model assumptions and should be distinguished from the observed database count itself.

## 7.7 Missing-person identification and disaster victim identification

Missing-person investigations may involve old, degraded, or very small samples from which autosomal DNA is limited or unavailable. Because mtDNA may remain recoverable in such material, it can support comparison with a living maternal relative when no direct reference exists [Amo19b, Cou21b]. A concordant result can link remains to a maternal lineage and help prioritize or support an identification, but ordinarily cannot distinguish among people who share that lineage. Its evidential weight depends on relevant population data and case propositions [Amo19b, Cou21b].

Operational MPS casework illustrates both added reach and interpretive limits. In a California Department of Justice missing-person program study, the assay produced usable control-region results for challenging samples that yielded little or no useful Sanger data, increasing the number available for comparison [Cue20]. Greater sensitivity also revealed low-level contamination or mixtures, some sequence data could not be reported, and inhibition impaired the assay in some samples.

The Romanov investigation is a foundational example of mtDNA used within a broader identification. Court’s review reports that the putative Tsarina’s mtDNA matched that of Prince Philip, a distant maternal relative. It also reports heteroplasmy in the putative Tsar’s remains that was verified in his brother Georgii, while clothing associated with the Tsar provided further confirmation [Cou21b]. As reported in this review, the case shows how maternal-line comparison can support identification while requiring corroborating evidence and careful treatment of sequence differences; a lineage match alone does not uniquely name an individual.

The ISFG disaster victim identification (DVI) recommendations place genetic comparisons within a traceable, multidisciplinary process. Post-mortem remains and family references should be separately documented, with pedigrees and biological relationships recorded; genetic data from participating laboratories should be compared systematically [Pri07]. Autosomal profiling is the standard approach, with mtDNA among the additional systems considered when it cannot provide sufficient information. Whenever possible, identifications should be anchored by a second identification modality or other corroborating data [Pri07]. A maternal-line association may identify the relevant family without resolving which relative the remains represent, guiding whether further comparisons or another identification modality are needed before formal identification [Cou21b].

## Summary

Mitochondrial DNA can remain informative when nuclear DNA is limited because many copies may persist in compromised material, but that recovery advantage does not confer person-level specificity. Reliable interpretation depends on validated sequencing workflows, contamination and artefact controls, method-aware treatment of heteroplasmy, consistent phylogenetic alignment, and population data suited to the comparison. A matching haplotype supports a maternal-line association whose weight depends on the propositions and reference data; in missing-person and DVI work, it is most useful as part of a broader identification process.
