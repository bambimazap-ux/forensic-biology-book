# Appendix B. Further Reading

## Chapter 0 Introduction to Forensic Science and Forensic Biology

- \[Rou22\] sets out the Sydney Declaration’s seven principles and frames forensic science as case-based inquiry into traces, uncertainty, and context. It is useful for understanding the field’s proposed foundations beyond laboratory technique.
- \[Kir63\] presents Kirk’s account of criminalistics and its historical focus on individualization. Read alongside later critiques, it shows how the field’s foundational claims have changed.
- \[Can25\] recounts Bertillon’s role in the Dreyfus affair and the disputed handwriting evidence. The paper illustrates the risks of expertise claims that exceed a practitioner’s relevant competence.
- \[Sak08\] separates a comparison finding from a conclusion that one source alone produced the trace. It develops the individualization fallacy and explains why empirical support is needed for source claims.
- \[Coo98\] introduces the hierarchy of source, activity, and offence propositions. It explains why a source-level finding cannot by itself establish the activity that produced it.
- \[Gil18\] presents ISFG guidance for evaluating DNA profile comparisons under sub-source or source propositions. It is a core reference on proposition formulation and the limits of DNA comparison conclusions.
- \[Gil20\] extends proposition-based evaluation to biological traces and activity-level questions. It discusses how transfer, persistence, recovery, and case information shape the assessment.
- \[Edm14\] compares how four adversarial jurisdictions receive incriminating expert evidence. Its review helps distinguish formal admissibility rules from how reliability disputes are handled in practice.
- \[Scu23\] proposes scientific guidelines for assessing the validity of forensic feature-comparison methods. It discusses research design, generalizability, replication, and the limits of checklist thinking.
- \[Coo19\] systematically reviews research on contextual information and forensic decision-making. Its findings are useful when read with attention to the uneven evidence base across disciplines.
- \[Had21\] surveys developments in forensic DNA analysis, including STR typing, mixture interpretation, and MPS. It offers a concise bridge from established profiles to newer methods.
- \[Bru18\] reviews massively parallel sequencing for forensic applications. It explains the added value of sequence variation and the need for validated analysis and consistent nomenclature.

## Chapter 1 Presumptive and Confirmatory Tests for Body Fluids

- \[Sij21\] A broad review of conventional and emerging body-fluid identification, including its role alongside DNA profiling and the limits of current methods. It provides a useful framework for distinguishing stain location, fluid markers, and interpretation.
- \[Str23b\] Compares common presumptive and immunochromatographic assays against several non-target biological materials. The study is valuable for understanding cross-reactions while noting that sample composition and the tested donor set constrain generalization.
- \[Cut24\] Reviews forensic immunochromatographic blood tests, their hemoglobin or glycophorin A targets, and causes of false-negative results. It is especially useful for understanding the hook effect and treatment-related interference.
- \[Gam20\] Reviews semen identification by AP, protein markers, and microscopy. It clearly distinguishes evidence of seminal plasma from the direct observation of spermatozoa.
- \[Wor18\] Evaluates Phadebas paper for saliva screening across temperature, substrates, and mixed-fluid samples. The results show why amylase activity should not be equated with saliva identification.
- \[Sta11\] Develops ELISA approaches using markers for saliva, sweat, and urine. Its comparison of fluid samples with prepared and aged stains illustrates the gap between promising markers and reliable stain detection.
- \[Ing18\] Reports a EUROFORGEN/EDNAP collaborative exercise on targeted mRNA sequencing. It documents both recurring marker patterns across laboratories and platform-related variability and ambiguity.
- \[Sal20\] Compares mRNA profiling in mock casework across laboratories using different workflows. Its low-input and mixture findings are useful for understanding marker dropout, unexpected signals, and the need for consistent interpretation.
- \[Swa25\] Develops a microbiome-based classification pipeline and tests it on controls and complex forensic-like samples. The paper shows potential while identifying unresolved needs in standardization, mixture interpretation, and casework validation.
- \[Ste12\] Uses mass-spectrometry proteomics to identify candidate protein markers across several body-fluid matrices. Its blind and case-sample testing also shows where urine and faeces identification remains less consistent.
- \[Sam22\] Demonstrates Bayesian networks for combining potentially conflicting observations in blood, saliva, and sperm examinations. It is useful for understanding how test results can be integrated while keeping data and model assumptions explicit.
- \[Bas22\] Tests a protocol for running multiple immunochromatographic assays and DNA profiling from the same eluate. The study is relevant to linking fluid identification with DNA while recognizing that results are specific to the tested workflow.

## Chapter 3 – DNA mixture interpretation

- \[Cla98\] develops a practical framework for recognizing and interpreting simple STR mixtures, including candidate genotype assignments and peak-area patterns. It also explains why reference profiles should follow an independent assessment of the questioned profile.
- \[Gil06\] sets out the ISFG recommendations for interpreting DNA mixtures, including contributor-number assumptions, mixture proportions, genotype combinations, and the use of likelihood-ratio frameworks. It is a key source on the limits of peak-based rules and the need for validation.
- \[Sci21\] presents SWGDAM guidelines for autosomal STR interpretation, covering mixture suitability, laboratory-established criteria, validation, documentation, and professional judgment. Its cautions are especially relevant to complex and stochastic-level mixtures.
- \[Hu14\] reviews developments in forensic interpretation of mixed DNA samples, including contributor-number estimation, major and minor components, reference profiles, and software. Read it as a review of the literature available at the time, not as a new validation study.
- \[Hic23\] examines variation among laboratories in assessments of mixture suitability and number of contributors. It distinguishes reproducibility and agreement with a known contributor count from the separate question of whether a count was discernible in the profile.
- \[Han11\] compares maximum allele count with a maximum-likelihood approach for estimating contributor number. Its simulation results show how performance depends on mixture complexity, allele-frequency information, and profile completeness.
- \[Per11\] evaluates allele-count approaches across loci and replicate amplifications in high- and low-template mixtures. The paper is useful for understanding overlap between contributor-count categories and the limits imposed by allele sharing and missing signal.
- \[Gil98b\] shows how allele peak areas can help assess candidate genotype combinations in simple two-person mixtures. Its method treats peak-area fit as support for an assignment, not as the probability that the assignment is true.
- \[Lyn18\] counts possible genotype allocations in mixtures and uses their growth to discuss manual deconvolution. It argues for caution with mixtures of three or more contributors while recognizing the exception of a clear major component.
- \[But18\] reviews the NIST MIX05 and MIX13 collaborative exercises and the variation seen in mixture interpretations. The authors caution that these study results describe specific exercises, not a universal casework error rate.
- \[Gil00\] examines STR interpretation at low template levels, including replicate amplification and consensus calls. It provides a primary-study basis for discussing the trade-off between reproducibility and stochastic variation.
- \[Ben11b\] compares n/2 consensus and pooled profiles for complex low-template mixtures. Its findings show that the approaches can recover different alleles and should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Chapter 2 – Profile characterization

- \[Con25\] systematically reviews forensic swab-material comparisons across biological sources and substrates. It shows why swab choice should be matched to the extraction workflow and why the evidence does not support one universal material ranking.
- \[Toz22\] reviews touch-DNA collection methods and compares single-swab, double-swab, and alternative approaches across varied study outcomes. Its synthesis is useful for understanding both the appeal of single-swabbing and the limits of cross-study rankings.
- \[Nog25\] compares forensic DNA elimination databases and contamination reporting across several European systems. It is useful for distinguishing database-based detection from the preventive controls needed throughout evidence handling.
- \[Vui11\] reports a collaborative comparison of differential-extraction protocols on simulated sexual-assault samples. The study illustrates variable enrichment, male-DNA loss, and why separated fractions should not be treated as pure.
- \[Cla21\] reviews conventional and emerging approaches to differential extraction in sexual-assault evidence. It compares their analytical aims with practical concerns such as manual handling, throughput, and automation.
- \[Fin22\] systematically reviews and meta-analyzes extraction methods for forensic bone and teeth. Its key contribution is to compare profiling success as well as DNA yield, while documenting limits caused by heterogeneous protocols and reporting.
- \[Ala12\] reviews sources and mechanisms of PCR inhibition in forensic samples and possible mitigation strategies. It is a useful reference for separating inhibition from low template or degradation and for recognizing trade-offs in cleanup and dilution.
- \[Ewi16\] presents developmental validation of a qPCR system that measures human and male DNA and includes degradation and inhibition indicators. It shows how multiple assay outputs can inform workflow choices without serving as a definitive prediction of STR success.
- \[Hud08\] develops a multiplex qPCR assay for human and male DNA quantification, degradation assessment, and inhibition monitoring. Its discussion helps explain the value and assay-specific limits of reading target ratios alongside an internal control.
- \[But06c\] reviews the genetics and use of core forensic STR loci, including allele variation and multiplex kits. It provides historical and technical context for shared marker sets, length-based calls, and their limitations.
- \[Sci21\] sets out guidance for autosomal STR interpretation, including electropherogram review, laboratory-validated criteria, and artifact assessment. It is a central reference for distinguishing analytical observations from interpretation decisions.
- \[Par16\] gives ISFG recommendations for nomenclature of STR alleles typed by massively parallel sequencing. It addresses sequence alignment and consistent designation, including compatibility with established length-based results.

## Chapter 4 Statistics of DNA Profile Analysis

- \[Bal94\] develops a framework for DNA match probabilities that accounts for population structure, relatedness, and finite reference databases. It is a foundational treatment of why a simple product-rule calculation can understate uncertainty.
- \[Bod16\] sets out quality-control recommendations for autosomal STR allele-frequency datasets, including documentation of sampling and laboratory methods and checks for data errors and duplicates. It clarifies how database curation supports, but does not guarantee, population representativeness.
- \[Cur02\] examines how finite database samples affect estimated DNA profile probabilities and compares ways to express that uncertainty. It is useful for distinguishing sampling uncertainty from population structure.
- \[Gil18\] gives ISFG guidance on evaluating DNA comparisons under sub-source propositions. Its central practical lessons concern case-specific propositions, conditioning assumptions, and explaining an LR without treating it as a probability of source identity.
- \[Gil06\] presents ISFG recommendations for interpreting and evaluating DNA mixtures. It develops explicit competing propositions and genotype-combination calculations while emphasizing assumptions about contributors and profile interpretation.
- \[Ge10\] describes pedigree likelihood calculations for missing-person identification that accommodate population substructure and mutation. It shows how reference profiles from several relatives can contribute jointly to an evaluation.
- \[Gil12\] recommends probabilistic evaluation when STR results may include drop-out or drop-in. The guidance stresses that event probabilities should be supported by validation data relevant to the laboratory’s method.
- \[Gil21c\] reviews EuroForMix, DNAStatistX, and STRmix and compares their broad modelling and computational approaches. It also discusses validation, user choices, and sources of variation in probabilistic-genotyping results.
- \[Cob16\] provides ISFG recommendations for validating software used in forensic-genetics calculations. It distinguishes provider and laboratory responsibilities and outlines checks for models, implementations, and intended casework use.
- \[Gil20\] gives ISFG guidance for evaluating biological findings under activity-level propositions. It explains why the level of the question matters and how relevant case information, transfer, persistence, recovery, and reporting limits should be addressed.
- \[Tay18b\] proposes a flexible Bayesian-network template for activity-level evaluations in forensic biology. The framework makes competing activities, transfer pathways, background explanations, findings, and assumptions explicit.
- \[Gil25b\] reports the ReAct multi-laboratory study of DNA recovery and activity-level evaluation. Its findings highlight that recovery and resulting evaluations may vary across laboratories, limiting direct transfer of data between them.

## Chapter 5 Forensic DNA Databases

- \[Ge14b\] reviews database purposes and design, including the balance between routine profile searching and broader marker strategies. Its proposed directions are prospective rather than a comparative evaluation of database effectiveness.
- \[San13b\] compares European inclusion and retention models with a measure of person–trace matches per included person. The study finds no clear performance advantage for more expansive models, while its proxy outcome and cross-country data limit conclusions about case resolution.
- \[Str19b\] systematically reviews empirical evidence on database detection, clearance, deterrence, and related purposes. It reports potential benefits but stresses that varied measures, designs, and database descriptions prevent broad or uniform conclusions.
- \[Piq19\] compares familial and moderate-stringency search policies and practices in the United States and England and Wales. It describes safeguards while highlighting privacy, equity, and weakly measured effectiveness concerns.
- \[Wei04\] analyzes full and partial profile matches, including how population structure and the scope of database searching affect expected associations. It is useful for understanding why profile similarity alone does not establish relationship or source contribution.
- \[Ben17c\] validates SmartRank, a likelihood-ratio method for searching databases with complex profiles. The paper shows how ranking can extend searches while emphasizing the limits imposed by mixture complexity, dropout, and the tested conditions.
- \[Noz24\] evaluates probabilistic-genotyping searches using laboratory mixtures and selected casework profiles. Its results show that candidate retrieval depends on thresholds, while casework leads lack independent ground truth and require case-specific validation.
- \[Mee19\] explains why database-match evaluation can depend on the propositions, prior information, and search protocol. It rejects a single universal formulation or mechanical correction based only on database size.
- \[Roh12\] examines how population structure and reference-frequency mismatch affect the distinction between relatives and unrelated people. The findings caution that familial-search rankings may be less reliable under population mismatch and for more distant relationships.
- \[Ama19\] reviews evidence on the UK National DNA Database’s investigative and broader public-security effects. It finds value in individual cases but notes that aggregate contribution and causal effect remain uncertain because of data and attribution limits.
- \[Too19\] analyzes Prüm’s two-stage cross-border DNA exchange and the difficulty of assessing its contribution to prosecutions. Incomplete reporting and varied national follow-up support the authors’ call for stronger transparency and accountability.
- \[Ube24\] proposes international governance priorities for forensic DNA databases, including privacy review, transparency, and oversight of new techniques. The recommendations are a starting point rather than a detailed or enforceable international regime.

## Chapter 6 Y-Chromosome Analysis

- \[Roe20\] sets out ISFG recommendations for interpreting Y-STR results, including frequency estimation, database use, propositions, rapidly mutating markers, and decision-based evaluation. It is the chapter’s main framework for cautious interpretation and reporting.
- \[Cou21\] provides a broad overview of Y-chromosome applications, marker systems, and lineage limitations. It is useful for connecting laboratory use with the interpretive limits of paternal inheritance.
- \[Kni22\] reviews Y-chromosome polymorphisms, including Y-STR haplotypes, Y-SNP haplogroups, identical-by-descent and identical-by-state sharing, and database interpretation. It clarifies what lineage markers can and cannot establish.
- \[And21b\] reviews the forensic value of Y-chromosome lineage evidence, with emphasis on mutation, relatedness, and database limits. It helps frame why a haplotype frequency is not automatically a case-specific match probability.
- \[And13\] presents the discrete Laplace approach to Y-STR haplotype-frequency estimation. It explains how relationships among observed haplotypes can inform estimates beyond exact-match counting.
- \[Cos25e\] reviews Y-STR database searching in sexual-crime investigations and its operational limitations. It is useful for distinguishing a search lead from a source conclusion.
- \[Job17\] provides a foundational overview of Y-chromosome variation and non-recombining inheritance. It supplies biological context for understanding linked paternal-line markers.
- \[Gus06b\] presents earlier ISFG recommendations on Y-STR interpretation. Read alongside the later guidance to see how interpretive recommendations developed.
- \[Wil15b\] describes an update to the Y-Chromosome Haplotype Reference Database. It helps explain the scope and structure of the population reference resource used in haplotype searches.
- \[Bal14\] examines rapidly mutating Y-STRs for distinguishing unrelated men and paternal relatives. It introduces the increased resolution and interpretive trade-offs of these markers.
- \[Ral22\] reports a large pedigree analysis of rapidly mutating Y-STR panels. It is useful for assessing how expanded marker sets perform across known paternal relationships.
- \[And17b\] critically examines matching Y profiles, rare haplotypes, and the number of paternal-line males who may share a profile. It highlights why database rarity alone does not settle the relevant alternative-source question.

## Chapter 7 Mitochondrial DNA Analysis

- \[Amo19b\] and \[Cou21b\] provide broad reviews of forensic mtDNA biology, methods, interpretation, and case applications. Together they offer an entry point to the field’s recovery advantages and lineage-based limits.
- \[Car26b\] reviews recent developments in forensic mtDNA methods and interpretation. It is useful for linking sequencing advances with workflow and evidential cautions.
- \[Par14c\] gives the ISFG DNA Commission’s revised recommendations for mtDNA analysis. It is the central source for nomenclature, heteroplasmy, quality control, and interpretation.
- \[Grz18\] provides an applied supplement to the international recommendations, with a stated regional remit. It is useful for laboratory safeguards and practical interpretation.
- \[Bra19\] validates a control-region MPS workflow. The study helps show why performance claims apply to the tested assay rather than to sequencing platforms in general.
- \[Hol21b\] evaluates ForenSeq control-region and whole-mitogenome workflows. It supports comparison of target scope, platform performance, and workflow-specific validation.
- \[Cue20\] reports MPS implementation in a missing-person program. The casework study illustrates both increased availability of mtDNA comparisons and limits from contamination, mixtures, and inhibition.
- \[Gaa20\] compares mtDNA findings in buccal references and rootless hairs using Sanger and MPS. It is especially relevant to tissue-dependent heteroplasmy and apparent hair–reference mismatches.
- \[Con22b\] examines whole-mitogenome heteroplasmy across known maternal relatives. Its findings show how comparison rules can affect exclusion decisions.
- \[Par07c\] describes EMPOP and its quality-control and population-data role. It provides a foundation for understanding curated mtDNA haplotype searches.
- \[Hub18\] presents SAM2 and phylogenetically informed alignment and search approaches. It explains why harmonized variant representation and alignment-independent searching can complement each other.
- \[Tem13\] demonstrates capture-based recovery of whole mitogenomes from fragmented remains. Read it as a feasibility study rather than a guarantee of equal performance across sample types or workflows.

## Chapter 8 Biogeographical Ancestry and Forensic DNA Intelligence

- \[Tve22\] reviews forensic ancestry inference and its interpretive limits. It is a useful framework for separating reference-based comparison from claims about identity or fine-scale origin.
- \[Ros03\] compares measures of ancestry-marker informativeness, including their dependence on the populations used for comparison. It helps explain why marker rankings are task-specific.
- \[Phi14\] describes the design of the EUROFORGEN Global AIM-SNP set and its approach to balancing differentiation across broad reference groups. It is useful for connecting marker selection with intended geographic resolution.
- \[Law18\] explains how sampling and model choices shape STRUCTURE and ADMIXTURE results. Read it to avoid treating plotted components as literal genealogies or natural population boundaries.
- \[Mog19\] evaluates ancestry assignment with the Precision ID panel and a method that can reject unsupported or ambiguous assignments. It illustrates the trade-off between forced rankings and inconclusive results.
- \[Per20\] evaluates ancestry estimates in admixed South American populations. It is especially relevant to uncertainty across marker sets and the difference between individual estimates and population-level patterns.
- \[Kay23b\] reviews forensic ancestry and phenotype approaches, including panel scope and interpretation. It provides a broad entry point for understanding how different marker sets support different inferences.
- \[Cha18\] presents the HIrisPlex-S system for predicting eye, hair, and skin-colour categories. It helps distinguish model outputs and category probabilities from a complete description of an individual.
- \[Phi26\] examines ancestry and phenotype analysis in Operation Minstead. The case illustrates how uncertain genetic results can be translated into overly precise investigative leads.
- \[Die26b\] reports an EDNAP collaborative exercise on interpretation and reporting of ancestry and phenotype outputs. Its inter-laboratory variation is most useful for considering complex and admixed profiles.
- \[Pou26\] sets out operational recommendations for forensic BGA use, including defining purpose and resolution and validating the full analytical capability. The recommendations are guidance, not a universal standard.
- \[Scu19c\] proposes an intelligence-cycle framework for using predictive DNA outputs. It emphasizes uncertainty, corroboration, and the distinction between a lead and identity evidence.

## Chapter 9 Forensic Epigenetics

- \[Vid18\] reviews forensic DNA methylation, its biological basis, and applications including age estimation, tissue typing, and twin differentiation. It is a broad entry point to the field’s promise and interpretive limits.
- \[Nau23b\] reviews chronological-age estimation from forensic DNA methylation, including marker selection, models, specimen effects, and uncertainty. It helps distinguish study-level performance from a case-specific prediction.
- \[Ref23\] discusses forensic epigenetics from a casework and validation perspective. It is useful for framing intended use, model evaluation, and cautious reporting.
- \[Sij21\] reviews tissue and body-fluid identification methods, including methylation and RNA approaches. Its comparisons clarify what each method targets and where mixture and specificity limits arise.
- \[Woz21\] reports VISAGE tissue-specific age models and their validation. The study is useful for comparing blood, buccal-cell, and bone performance within a defined workflow.
- \[Hei25c\] reports an inter-laboratory evaluation of VISAGE age-prediction methods. It shows why transfer across laboratories is a distinct test from a held-out evaluation within model development.
- \[Lee21\] presents a collaborative evaluation of SNaPshot methylation measurements. Its findings help explain how instrument and assay conditions can affect cross-laboratory comparability.
- \[Maa19\] develops and externally evaluates a methylation panel for smoking-status classification in population cohorts. It supports discussion of category-dependent performance while not validating crime-scene trace testing.
- \[Ger24\] reviews forensic epigenetic applications and sample-related limitations. It is especially useful for considering tissue effects, confounding, mixtures, and the maturity of emerging applications.
- \[Fre20b\] compares methylation measurements across EpiTYPER, pyrosequencing, SNaPshot, and sequencing platforms. It illustrates that platform agreement can depend on the loci and method tested.
- \[Vid17\] reports a proof-of-concept study of methylation differences between monozygotic twins using reference and trace-type blood samples. Its results show why candidate markers need testing in the material intended for casework.
- \[Pal26\] examines ethical concerns in forensic epigenetics, including sensitive health- and lifestyle-adjacent inferences. It provides a framework for considering privacy safeguards and function creep.

## Chapter 10 — Forensic Investigative Genetic Genealogy (FIGG)

- \[Kli21\] reviews FIGG methods and practice, from SNP data generation through database searching and genealogical follow-up. It is the central technical and operational overview for the chapter.
- \[Vri21\] evaluates how compromised DNA affects SNP-microarray genotyping and kinship classification. It is useful for seeing why assay performance depends on sample and workflow rather than a universal input threshold.
- \[Til21b\] documents the Swedish double-murder investigation, including repeated efforts to obtain usable SNP data, genealogical narrowing, and direct STR comparison. It clearly illustrates the separation between a family lead and confirmation.
- \[Gue21\] addresses common misconceptions about investigative genetic genealogy and explains its lead-generating role and the need for conventional follow-up. It is a concise guide to keeping the inference boundary clear.
- \[Erl18\] examines distant-relative matching and models how database coverage may affect identity inference. Its assumptions and ancestry composition are important when interpreting population-level projections.
- \[Gre19\] describes genetic-genealogy practice in cold-case and active investigations, including match analysis and family-tree research. It is useful for understanding the investigative work between a database match and a candidate lead.
- \[Gue18\] studies public views of police access to genealogy databases in the context of the Golden State Killer investigation. Its sampling limits make it a useful example of why stated survey support is not a population-wide mandate.
- \[Scu19b\] analyzes the policy and regulatory implications of using consumer genealogy data in forensic investigations. It provides a framework for understanding relational privacy and the limits of participant consent.
- \[Edg19b\] examines privacy attacks that use uploads and shared-segment information in genealogy databases. The paper helps distinguish demonstrated technical possibilities from claims about routine exposure of customer genomes.
- \[Wic25\] sets out updated NTVIC recommendations for FIGG eligibility, proportionality, confirmation, data handling, and program governance. It is useful as professional guidance while remaining distinct from binding law across jurisdictions.
- \[Dah24\] uses focus groups to examine views on FIGG purposes, database notice, opt-in or opt-out settings, notification, and accountability. Its qualitative design illuminates reasons for support and concern, not their prevalence nationwide.
- \[Dow22\] compiles publicly confirmed FIGG case applications and outcomes. It is useful for understanding reported uses, while its lack of a complete denominator limits conclusions about overall success or equity.

## Chapter 11 — Fingerprints: Fundamentals and Development

- \[Glo23\] proposes a developmental account in which epithelial signaling and waves of ridge formation shape friction-ridge patterns. It is useful for understanding current mechanistic ideas while keeping in view the limits of direct causal evidence in human fetal development.
- \[Mon19\] distinguishes permanence of skin features from persistence of impressions across time and capture conditions. It helps clarify why broad ridge flow and finer visible features need not have the same stability.
- \[Rob22\] systematically reviews studies of fingermark residue composition and finds substantial variation in donors, collection, substrates, storage, analytes, and methods. It is a strong starting point for judging why no single chemical profile can be assumed for an individual mark.
- \[Bec26d\] reviews fingermark composition, visualization, and casework conditions. It is useful for connecting laboratory findings with the limits of generalizing from small or idealized samples.
- \[Bec20\] surveys fingermark development methods and substrate-dependent choices. It provides a broad reference for how residue location and surface behavior shape method selection.
- \[Jel09b\] explains the chemistry and visualization of amino-acid reagents used on porous materials. It is especially useful for comparing the visible and luminescent signals produced by ninhydrin, DFO, and IND.
- \[Sod16\] describes the chemistry and application of physical developer, a silver-based process distinct from amino-acid reagents. It helps explain why this method may be considered for selected wet or aged porous items, while paper composition can limit performance.
- \[Mar14f\] compares two reagent sequences on paper types and older examination booklets. Its differing controlled and pseudo-operational results show why a sequence should be judged against the specific substrate and trial conditions.
- \[War07\] uses model experiments to examine the polymerization chemistry involved in cyanoacrylate fuming. It offers a mechanistic account while making clear that model-residue results do not establish a complete explanation for every natural mark.
- \[Bum16\] reviews small-particle reagent for wet and moist nonporous surfaces, including formulation-specific applications. It is useful for understanding the method’s target and why results should not be generalized across substrates or adhesive types.
- \[Dov25\] reviews forensic vacuum metal deposition, its reported specialist applications, and proposed mechanisms. It is particularly useful for seeing that the method can be applied to challenging materials even though its contrast-producing mechanism remains unsettled.
- \[Sea12\] discusses how fingermark method performance should be evaluated across stages from model-material screening to operational trials. It helps distinguish promising early results from evidence that supports casework use.

## Chapter 12 — Fingerprint Comparison, Validity and Automated Identification (AFIS/ABIS)

- \[Bec20, Bec22\] review fingermark comparison, reporting formats, conclusion scales, and the limits of generalization. They are broad maps to current debates, not validation studies.
- \[Bec25\] addresses the selection of proficiency tests for the fingerprint field, complementing empirical studies of examiner performance.
- \[Ule11\] reports a black-box study of latent-print decisions against known-source comparisons. It shows how response categories and error definitions shape measured performance.
- \[Ule12\] examines repeatability and reproducibility of latent-print decisions, helping distinguish agreement from correctness.
- \[Ule14\] studies how examiner-marked features relate to individualization decisions; its annotations are interpretations, not objective feature counts or ground-truth accuracy measures.
- \[Hic25b, Rob24\] address casework-like test design and how case selection, response bias, inconclusive outcomes, prevalence, and analysis choices affect performance estimates.
- \[Cou09, Cor16\] present influential assessments of the scientific basis for latent-print source conclusions. Read them together to distinguish critiques of the evidence base from PCAST’s bounded finding of foundational validity.
- \[Neu12, Abr13\] develop and review statistical fingerprint-comparison models, including their assumptions about feature configurations, distortion, examiner variation, and reference data.
- \[Swo18b, Neu20b\] pair FRStat’s developers’ account with a critique of interpreting its error-tail ratio as an evidential likelihood ratio; \[Neu20c\] separately critiques score-based likelihood ratios and the evidential meaning of matcher scores.
- \[Car20b, Bus22, Mor24b\] examine expanded conclusion scales and a proof-of-concept method for mapping primary-examiner ACE categories to Bayes factors; the latter uses a small dataset.
- \[Cao17, Ind09, Ind11, Wil04e\] cover matcher architecture and NIST evaluations of fingerprint and latent-search systems, including the Phase II selection-bias caveat and examiner-marked extended features. \[Aya23\] surveys multimodal biometric fusion relevant to the broader ABIS context.
- \[Tay12, Dro06b, Dro12b\] address documentation, candidate-list handling, contextual information, and human–technology interaction in latent-print work.
